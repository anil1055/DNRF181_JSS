**ABSTRACT**

*Context: Reliable effort estimation is essential for better*
*management of Web projects, hence the need to identify what are*
*the key factors that affect effort estimates for new Web projects*
*and how they are inter-related. Objective:* *This paper improves*
*our understanding of Web effort estimation using as basis the*
*knowledge of Web effort estimation experts. Method: We*
*employed qualitative research with the participation of four*
*different Web development companies in Manaus (Brazil) using*
*semi-structured interviews for data collection; our data analysis*
*was carried out using Grounded Theory-based procedures to*
*identify and combine factors affecting the estimation effort of Web*
*projects. Results: We identified four main groupings (categories)*
*of factors - Web project, Web development complexity, Web*
*development team, and Clients. Each of these groupings contains*
*a set of factors that impact upon Web effort estimation.*
*Conclusions: This is the first time that qualitative research is*
*employed in the field of Web effort estimation to further*
*understand and help improve this process. In addition, some of*
*the factors found had never been identified in any of the previous*
*studies in this field, thus suggesting that the use of Grounded*
*Theory-based procedures may provide a way to enrich our*
*understanding of the phenomenon under investigation via the*
*identification of factors that overlap and also complement those*
*from previous studies.*

Given that Web development is a rapidly and constantly growing
industry [3], we believe that it is important to understand the Web
effort estimation phenomenon given its importance to managing
Web projects. In addition, such understanding can also help Web
companies in their project management decisions.

To understand effort estimation for Web projects, previous studies
have developed models that use as input, factors such as the size
of a Web application, and cost drivers (e.g. tools, developer’s
quality, team size), and provide an effort estimate as output. The
differences between these studies were the number and type of
size measures used, choice of cost drivers and occasionally the
techniques employed to build the effort estimation models.

Mendes and Counsell [20] were the first, back in 2000, to
investigate this field by building a model that used machinelearning techniques with data from student-based Web projects,
and size measures harvested late in the project’s life cycle.
Mendes and collaborators also carried out a series of consecutive
studies (e.g. [20][21][22][23][24]) where models were built using
multivariate regression and machine-learning techniques using
data on industrial Web projects. Later they also proposed and
validated size measures harvested early in the project’s life cycle,
and therefore better suited to resource estimation [23].

Other researchers have also investigated effort estimation for Web
projects, and some examples are as follows: Reifer [28] proposed
an extension of the COCOMO model, and a single size measure
harvested late in the project’s life cycle. None were validated
empirically. This size measure was later used by Ruhe et al. [30],
who further extended a software engineering hybrid estimation
technique to Web projects, using a small data set of industrial
projects, mixing expert judgment and multivariate regression.
Later, Baresi et al. [4], and Mangia et al. [16] investigated effort
estimation models and size measures for Web projects based on a
specific Web development method, namely the W2000. Finally,
Costagliola et al. [9] compared two types of Web-based size
measures for effort estimation. A recent systematic literature
review on Web resource estimation [3] (see Section 2) showed
that none of the previous studies employed qualitative research to
understanding the Web effort estimation phenomenon. However,
there are numerous examples in Software & Web Engineering in
which the use of qualitative research in isolation or combined with
quantitative research, provided a rich understanding of the
phenomenon being investigated [7] [31] [33].

**3.** **RESEARCH METHODOLOGY**

We employed a qualitative research methodology aiming to
understand what factors contribute for better effort estimation,
based on the expert opinion of practitioners directly involved in
effort estimation activities.
The data employed in our research was gathered by means of
semi-structural interviews with four professionals from different
Web development companies in Manaus (Brazil). Furthermore,
we employed procedures from Grounded Theory (GT) [34] while
analyzing the data.

The subsections detailed next detail the activities relating to our
data collection and analysis.

**3.1** **Data Collection**

Four experts working respectively at four different Web
companies participated in this research. Two subjects had each 10
years of experience; the other two had 6 and 4 years of
experience, respectively. Moreover, all subjects participated
actively in the estimation of effort for their companies’ new Web
projects.

We employed interviews as means to elicit those practitioners’
tacit knowledge with regard to how they estimate effort for new
projects, focusing on the factors they find important to determine
estimates, how those factors are inter-related, and whenever
applicable, whether a factor has a positive of negative influence
upon an estimate.

The following steps were carried out to accomplish our research:

- Definition of goals and selection of techniques to be used as
part of this research;

- Selection of practitioners who have experience estimating
effort for Web projects;

- Conducting interviews with practitioners using semistructured and open questions. All interviews were been
recorded;

- After each interview, we performed the following activities:

`o` Transcription of the interviews’ recorded content;
`o` Coding the data from the transcripts, comprising: (a)
the identification of relevant codes within the context
of Web effort estimation, (b) the analysis of the
relationships between codes, and (c) the identification
of categories to group the codes previously found;

- Data analysis, in which we listed the identified categories
that can influence the effort estimation of Web projects;

- Synthesis of categories in a list of factors that affect effort
estimation, details of each category from the data gathered;
selection of interviews‘ representative excerpts (quotations)
for each category;

- Data interpretation – analysis of each factor of influence;

Note that each interview ranged from 27 to 105 minutes. This
difference in duration can be related to the individual

characteristics of each subject (e.g. taking longer to answer
questions, be more verbose).
In order to meet ethical needs, we created a consent form to notify
the subjects about research procedures and confidentiality, and to
ask for their consent.


We used semi-structured and open questions to allow a detailed
investigation about the context in which the interviewees were
immersed. Such procedure allowed us to make explicit the
interviewees’ tacit knowledge. During the interviews, the experts
described their experience and approach to problem solving when
estimating effort for Web projects.
Before carrying out the interviews, we sent e-mails to potential
subjects inviting them to participate in our research, and also
disclosing that their interviews would be recorded for research
purposes only.

Table 1 shows the questions employed in the interviews. Such
questions could also lead to further questions depending on the
interviewees’ answers. All the interviews were carried out by the
first author, who encouraged the participants to speak freely while
answering the questions.

**Table 1. Questions used in the Interview**

Each interview had its full transcription created. By using the
transcriptions, we were able to thoroughly analyze the
interviewees’ knowledge. We must point out that all the observed
data was exclusively obtained from the interviews.
We did not characterize the interviews transcriptions in order to
preserve participants’ identity. We present the qualitative analysis
based on procedures of GT in the following subsection.

**3.2** **Data Analysis**
The qualitative analysis performed in this work is based on
procedures from the Grounded Theory (GT) method. Grounded
Theory (aka Data Grounded Theory) uses a set of systematic data
collection and analysis procedures to generate, prepare, and
validate substantive theories on essentially social phenomena, or
on wide social processes [11]. Although the purpose of the
Grounded Theory Method is the construction of substantive
theories, its use does not necessarily need to remain restricted
only to researchers who have this research goal. According to
Strauss and Corbin [34], the researcher may use only some of its
procedures to meet one’s research goals.


14


Grounded Theory is based on the coding idea which is the process
of analyzing the data [34]. Concepts (or codes) and categories are
identified during the coding stage. The coding process can be
divided into three stages: open, axial, and selective coding. A
concept name is a phenomenon that has the interest of the
researcher; it abstracts an event, an object, an action or interaction
that has a meaning to the researcher [34]. Categories are clusters
of concepts joined in a higher degree of abstraction.

Open coding involves the breakdown, analysis, comparison,
conceptualization, and the categorization of the data. The created
codes can be classified as: (a) first-order coding, which is directly
associated to the citations (referred to as *in-vivo* coding); and (b)
abstract or theoretical codes, which are associated to other codes
without necessarily being connected to some citation. Finally,
during open coding, categories are created to cluster the codes and
reduce the number of units the researcher will work with.

After the identification of conceptual categories by the open
coding, the axial coding examines the relations between the
categories. The relations between codes can be defined by the
very researcher. According to Strauss and Corbin [34], these
relations form what the authors call a paradigm: causal conditions,
new conditions, consequences, and action strategies/interactions.

Finally, the selective coding realizes all the process refinements
by identifying the core category with which all others are related.
The core category should be able to integrate all other categories
and to express the social process’ essence. We decided not to elect
a core category herein because a Grounded Theory rule is the
circularity between the collection and analysis stages until the
theoretical saturation is reached [34]. Therefore we decided to
postpone the selective coding phase. For this reason, we do not
claim that we applied the GT method, only some specific
procedures.
Just after the transcription of each interview, we started the
codification process. While we analysed the data contained within
the interview transcription, codes associated with textual parts
were created, as shown in Figure 5. The codes related with
citations in each interview transcription were reviewed with three
other researchers, who verified those codes and categories in order
to audit the coding process. This was done in order to mitigate the
bias eventually caused by the participation of a single researcher
in the codification process.

**Figure 5. Example of open coding - Code and citation**

**4.** **OUR RESULTS**

In this Section we detail the results of our qualitative research. We
have identified 35 factors, which were grouped into four
categories: *Web Project*, *Web development team*, *Clients, and Web*
*development complexity* . All the identified factors are displayed in
Table 2. Some of these factors had either a positive or negative
influence towards effort depending on its conditions. For instance,
if an employee has a high level of experience in the project
business area, the factor “Experience in the business area” could
be a positive influence in a Web project effort estimation in this
context. However, we could also identify factors that the experts


always consider as “positive factors” or “negative factors”
inherently.
A positive factor represents a factor that facilitates the process of
effort estimation, and as a consequence increases the probability
of obtaining a more accurate estimate; conversely, a negative
factor represents a factor that hinders the effort estimation
process, making it more difficult to obtain an accurate effort
estimate.

The factors that were identified represent those that the experts
believed to affect effort estimates for Web projects, and therefore
need to be taken into account during an effort estimation process.
In the context of Web development, the identification of important
factors is crucial, such that projects can be delivered on time and

within budget. In addition, it also helps with managing projects
properly under existing constraints such as short time to market,
and a very dynamic environment [25].

**4.1** **Web Project**

We found some factors influencing an effort estimate that are
related to the product or, in other words, the Web application
itself. We identified, such as:

**Product’s Innovation level –** according with one of the subjects,
the innovation level of a Web project can generate a larger effort
in the development and, therefore, it must be considered in the
effort estimation process. This is illustrated in the following
quotation.

*“The project’s innovation level, not necessarily radical*
*innovation, but an innovation, even if it is an incremental*
*innovation or something, for instance… not having a ready*
*component is a factor that we consider.” - Interviewee 3*

**Integration with existing Systems –** according to two subjects, it
is very difficult to estimate Web projects that require a lot of
communication with devices and other applications (e.g.
Integration with social networks).

*“When you have a Web project, you have a lot of*
*communication with devices, other applications or other*
*databases that are external to your application. In this situation,*
*you estimate both the time that you will spend to integrate the*
*different applications and the time you will spend implementing it,*
*and this can increase the cost and the effort depending of the*
*complexity of what you have to do” - Interviewee 2*

*“...the integration with existing System is a very difficult*
*task. (...) this estimative was more like a ‘belief’, because*
*sometimes we had no idea about how to do the integration…”-*

*Interviewee 4*

**Technical factors** were also cited. These factors were related to
the Web product, such as: access to database tables, how many

*“...because what influences are the four factors that I told*

**4.2** **Web development team**

Regarding the development team, we found factors that positively
and negatively affect the estimation accuracy: facilitating or
hindering the process. The factors we have identified as being of
positive influence are:

**Team collaboration –** according to one of the interviewees, if the
interaction and exchange of information and experience between
the members of a development team increase, so will their
productivity. Hence, this is a factor that affects the effort
estimation. This factor was identified based on the following
information:

*“If we are in a team that exchanges more information,*
*experiences,* *then, we'll probably have a much higher productivity*
*and these factors are very important ”* - *Interviewee 2*

**Knowing the development team** : the knowledge that a project
manager has regarding the productivity of a development team,
and also relating to the individual characteristics of each team
member (with their skills) can have an effect upon an effort
estimate. If the knowledge is high the likelihood that the effort
estimate will be more accurate increases.

*“I need to know very well my team, to know who is working*
*with me, the characteristics of each team member, and to know*
*how can I use this knowledge to minimize the time” - Interviewee*

**4.3** **Client**

Our results also identified several factors relevant for effort

estimation that are related to clients. When we know the client we
can indentify characteristics that can facilitate the effort
estimation process:

**Client that has his/her procedures mapped:** in this case, the

In this Section, we observe that the qualitative research helped us
identify categories and relationships of factors that influence the
effort estimation in Web projects. In the next Section we present
the threats to the validity of our study.

**5.** **THREATS TO VALIDITY**

As in all studies, there were various threats that could affect the
validity of our results. The main threat to the validity of the results
is the small number of interviewees who participated in the study.
As there were only four interviewees (from four different
companies), we cannot generalize our results to all contexts.
Therefore, it is necessary to expand this research, including a
larger number of participating professionals and companies.

Additionally, because the data and the subsequent analysis are
qualitative, there is no scope to call upon statistical arguments for
generalization of any results. However, it is noteworthy that the
interviewees are representative cases of effort estimation experts
from four different Web development companies. And two of the
experts work with globally distributed projects.

Another threat to the validity of the results is the subjectivity of
the data classification, since the qualitative analysis was
performed by the first author. We used the Grounded Theory
procedure in order to mitigate this threat, given that the GT
requires the entire analysis to be grounded in the data collected.
Additionally, the analysis process was performed along with three
other researchers, to encourage a better validation of the
interpretations through the mutual agreement of the researchers.

**6.** **CONCLUSIONS**

We have identified, via a qualitative research, factors that affect
the estimation of effort for Web projects. These factors were
elicited from knowledge obtained from four Web effort estimation
experts using semi-structured interviews, and Grounded Theory
procedures over the documents with the transcription of the
performed interviews.

The factors that were identified were grouped into four different
categories, to facilitate the understanding of the phenomenon
being investigated. These categories are: Web Project, Web
development team, Client, and Web Development Complexity.


Note that, depending on the characteristics of a Web project, it is
possible that some categories become more relevant than others
when estimating effort; however they all should be taken into
account when deciding upon an effort estimate for a new Web
project.

The explicitation of the tacit knowledge from experts on the
factors they consider important, when estimating effort for Web
projects, enables the use of such explicit knowledge to improve
effort estimation decision making, and ultimately the estimates
that are prepared [18]. Mendes [18] has also explicitated the tacit
knowledge from experts aiming to understand and improve Web
effort estimation; however she employed quantitative research.
Her findings showed that the explicit representation of Web effort
prediction factors was viewed quite positively by the participating
company given that they could use it for decision making,
discussions with clients, and also with the development team
whenever they needed team members to provide effort estimates.

In addition, some of the factors identified herein differ from those
in [18], thus suggesting that the use of different techniques and
types of research may lead to a better understanding of a
complementary differ from those identified herein, and their
comparison and combination is the focus of our future work. We
also plan to interview other experts from other Web companies
using qualitative research as means to enrich our existing model
and understanding via the identification of further factors and,
optionally, categories.

With regard to the use of Grounded-theory principles, we did not
reach the theoretical saturation of this phenomenon, because it
would be necessary for new rounds of experiments, to thereby
identify the core category. Then, as future research we can
propose the execution of new interviews within new experiments.
Such experiments should be carried out in different contexts
(cities or countries) to gather further information.

It is noteworthy that qualitative research, such as the one
presented here, aims at the identification of concepts and the
relationships between them, leading to a greater understanding of
how a phenomenon of interest occurs. Therefore, the quality of
data collected is more important than the amount of evidence
gathered [32].

Each qualitative study contributes to advancing the state of art in a
research area, providing evidence and hypothesis that can be later
tested using quantitative methods [30]. Gathering further evidence
enables us to improve the corpus of evidence on Web effort
estimation. We also plan to conduct further research to investigate
in what scenarios the factors from a specific category are more
important than the others.
