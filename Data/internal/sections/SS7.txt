I. I NTRODUCTION

Effective resource management is one of the requisites of
successful software development. It enables managerial
decisions to be made with regards to cost, quality and
scheduling tradeoffs inherent in the software lifecycle [1, 2].
Central to this is resource estimation, which has traditionally
focused on effort estimation - the process by which software
development effort is forecasted. Effort predictors are typically


measures of “problem size” along with other factors that have a
bearing on a project’s outcome [3].

Given that the Web development industry is rapidly
growing [4], research geared towards enabling Web
development companies to make sound managerial decisions
would be worthwhile. To this end a systematic literature
review (SLR) was recently carried out to provide an in depth
view into Web resource estimation [4]. In addition to
confirming that research in this domain has focused on effort
estimation using size measures as predictors, the SLR showed
that despite the number of effort estimation techniques
investigated, there is no consensus as to which is the best.
Accuracy results were found to vary widely depending not only
on the estimation technique used, but also on the dataset
investigated, and the validation procedure and accuracy
measure/s utilized [5]. Furthermore, a crucial source of bias is
the statistical procedure that is used in the process of promoting
a certain prediction model against a comparative one [6, 7].

One possible approach to these problems is to use estimates
generated by ensembles of prediction techniques. This involves
combining the predictions of a number of individual
estimators. Kocaguneli et al. [8] demonstrated that a set of
individual estimation techniques can be divided into a
“minority” subset of “superior” techniques and a “majority”
subset of “inferior” techniques. They found that ensembles
made from these superior techniques outperformed all
individual techniques, and more importantly, maintained their
performance over 20 datasets and several accuracy measures.

Towards this direction, Mittas and Angelis [9] pointed out
that the notion of the “best” estimation model may have to be
reconsidered in *Software Cost Estimation* (SCE) experimental
studies, since usually there is not just a globally optimal model
but rather a subset of best estimation models. A simplistic
approach of combining models is to rank the single techniques
according to a measure of predictive performance and then to
utilize a subset of them. On the other hand, the critical decision
that one should take is the identification of the models that are
significantly superior from all the others, in a statistical sense.
In [9] a formal statistical methodology is proposed from the

broad class of *multiple comparisons procedures*, which is able
to rank and cluster several prediction models into nonoverlapping homogenous groups.

The abovementioned studies have been conducted in the
domain of general software development. This differs from
web development in a number of areas including the personnel
involved in the development process, the intrinsic
characteristics of the software, and the audience for which they
are developed. A more detailed discussion of the difference
between general software development and web development
is provided in [25]. Due to these differences it is not possible to
assume that the findings of these previous studies can be
generalized to Web effort estimation. Therefore the goal of this
research is to investigate whether such ensembles of techniques
would be as effective for Web effort estimation, which has not
been done before [4].

Two approaches will be used: The first will involve
creating ensembles by replicating the work done by
Kocaguneli et al. [8] using the Tukutuku dataset of Web
projects. The second approach by Mittas and Angelis [9], also
using the Tukutuku dataset, will involve building ensembles
incorporating a multiple hypothesis testing procedure, namely
the *Scott-Knott* algorithm. Finally, we have to mention that the
identification of the best cluster of prediction models according
to the latter approach is conducted through the utilization of a
fully automated statistical tool, namely StatREC [10, 11].

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2
provides details on the original study by Kocaguneli et al. [8]
as well as our replication. Section 3 will briefly describe the
approach by Mittas and Angelis [9]. Section 4 details our
results, and Section 5 will conclude the paper with a discussion
of our findings and comments on future work.

II. T HE R EPLICATION

The following section will cover our replication of the
study performed by Kocaguneli et al. [8]. In order to provide
context for our replication we will first discuss the original
study upon which our replication is based [12]. We will then
highlight how our replication differs from the original study.

*A.* *The Original Study*

*1)* *Research Aim:* When it comes to software effort
estimation there is no consensus as to which of the numerous

estimation techniques investigated is the best (i.e. most
accurate) [8, 9]. Kocaguneli et al. [8] aimed to demonstrate
that a better option to looking for a single best estimation
technique would be to consider estimates generated by
ensembles of multiple techniques; specifically ensembles of
“superior” techniques as will be discussed in more detail next.

*2)* *Methodology:* The research methodology used can be
summarized into the following three steps [8]:

� A large number of effort estimation techniques were

run.

� These techniques were evaluated and then sorted using

a predefined set of performance measures.

Ensembles combine the estimates provided by two or more
solo techniques into a single estimate [8]. Three simple
combination schemes were utilized whereby a multimethod
estimate was obtained by taking the mean, median, and inverse
rank weighted mean (IRWM) of the estimates obtained from
the solo techniques that make up the ensemble.

The 90 solo techniques were evaluated using a set of
performance measures or error measures that provide
information on the quality of a prediction. The *absolute*
*residual* (AR) (a residual is the difference between the estimate
and the actual value) forms the basis for all performance
measures used. The MRE ( *magnitude of relative error* ) is
calculated by considering the absolute residual relative to the
actual value. The absolute residual can also be considered
relative to the estimate ( *magnitude of relative error relative to*
*the estimate* or MER). The *balanced relative error* (BRE) and
*inverted balanced relative error* (IBRE) are calculated by
dividing the absolute residual by the minimum and maximum,
respectively, of the estimate and the actual values.

In all seven performance measures were considered: the
mean absolute residual (MAR), mean and median MRE
(MMRE and MdMRE), the percentage of estimates with an
MRE of 25% or less (PRED(25)), the mean MER (MMER),
the mean BRE (MBRE) and the mean IBRE (MIBRE) [8].
These performance measures were selected on the basis that
they had been previously used in the literature on effort
estimation.


� Ensembles were built using a subset of best estimation

techniques.

The effort estimation techniques investigated in [8] were
categorized into “solo” techniques, and ensembles of these solo
techniques referred to as “multimethods”. A solo technique is
run on a dataset by first pre-processing the data (referred to as
the pre-processing option), followed by generating effort
estimates on this data by running a learner (an effort estimation
algorithm) using leave-one-out cross-validation. Ten preprocessing options and nine learners were used, giving a total
of 90 solo techniques. These learners were selected on the basis
that they had been previously investigated in the software
engineering effort estimation literature, and that they made
different assumptions about the data. Table 1 provides a
summary of these pre-processors and learners.

The win, tie and loss counters were then used to identify
the best solo techniques, i.e., the ones with which to build the
effort estimation ensembles. The 90 solo techniques were first
ranked according to their number of losses - from best (least
number of losses) to worst (most number of losses). The solo
techniques were then also ranked according to their number of
wins and number of wins-losses. These additional rankings
were used to assess ranking instability by enabling the
calculation of the maximal change in rank, *�r*, of a solo learner

[8]. For example, if a solo learner was ranked 3rd for losses,
5th for wins and 7th for wins-losses, then *�r = 4* .

It was found that the top 13 solo techniques, as ranked by
their number of losses, also had low *�r* values [8]. In addition,
these solo techniques had noticeably lower *�r* values than the
solo techniques that followed. These techniques were therefore
deemed “superior” and were used to build ensembles. A total
of 12 ensembles were made using the top two, top four, top
eight and top thirteen solo methods, with individual estimates
being combined in one of three ways - mean, median and
IRWM, as mentioned previously. The 12 ensembles were then
ranked along with the solo techniques using the same
evaluation process.

*B.* *Our Replication*

We performed a dependent replication [20] with the goal of
investigating whether the methodology used in [8] would result
in similar findings when evaluated with Web project data. To
this end we used:

� The same 90 solo techniques.

� The same seven performance measures.

� The same round-robin evaluation using leave-one-out

cross-validation.

� The same three combination schemes for building

ensembles.

We were fortunate to be able to consult with the first author
of [8] and be able to use the same code for the nine learners
and most of the pre-processors. Whatever we coded was done
with strict adherence to the methodology described in [8] with
the author of the original study, once again, kindly clearing up
any queries we had. All of our programming was done on the
64 bit Windows version of MATLAB R2012a.

In terms of Web project data we did not have access to 20
datasets, which was the number of datasets used by the original
study. Instead we used the Tukutuku dataset, which as far as
we know, is a unique repository of Web project data. Tukutuku
was also used by our second approach which will be discussed
in the next section.

*1)* *The Tukutuku dataset:* Tukutuku is a database of
project data on Web hypermedia and software applications
obtained from companies around the globe [17]. Each of the
195 projects in this database is characterized by a set of 25
variables related to Web applications and their development.
While the data in Tukutuku were collected from completed
projects, the variables were chosen on the basis that they could
be measured early in the development cycle [17].

For both approaches used by this study, only numerical
variables were considered as effort predictors. As with [17, 18,
19], we felt that using the Tukutuku categorical variables:

� Would not be of value for this analysis.

� Would require the use of dummy variables reducing the

degrees of freedom for analysis.

along with summary statistics is provided in Table II.

III. S ELECTIVE F USION T HROUGH M ULTIPLE C OMPARISONS

P ROCEDURE

The methodology described in the previous section
provides a mechanism in order to rank alternative prediction
models based on different accuracy measures. The final
ranking is derived from an algorithm, which collects the
number of wins, ties and losses for each model compared to all
the remaining, through the non-parametric Wilcoxon test.
Finally, the ensembles are evaluated via an arbitrary choice of
the top *M* �{,8,4,212,16} solo-methods.

Recently, Mittas and Angelis [9] discussed the problem of
the divergent results reported in software cost estimation (SCE)
experimental studies concentrating on the lack of wellestablished statistical procedures. Specifically, they focused on
the “ *multiple comparisons problem* ”, which can be defined as
the simultaneous statistical testing of multiple prediction
models and the identification of the best group. The latter is
certainly of primary interest especially from the project
managers’ point of view, since the crucial issue is to decide
how many of the alternative models are evidently the best,
meaning that they present statistically significant differences
from the comparative ones. Furthermore, they analytically
described the “ *error inflation* ” phenomenon and the misleading
results that one can derive, in cases where a large number of
pair-wise comparisons are conducted.

To overcome the problem accompanying multiple
hypotheses testing, a statistical framework was proposed [9],
which is able to rank, and more importantly, to cluster the
alternative prediction models into non-overlapping groups. The
latter is an interesting property of the approach, since
traditional statistical procedures provide overlapping groups of
models causing major interpretation problems in decisionmaking.

Describing briefly, the methodology is settled on a wellestablished statistical area, namely *design of experiments*
(DOE), which can provide a systematic way to plan, design
and analyze an experimental study in order to obtain valid and
meaningful results. Based on the DOE and the results of *one-*
*way analysis of variance* (ANOVA), the *Scott-Knott* (SK)
algorithm [14], an efficient multiple comparisons procedure,
manages to rank the alterative cost estimation models, identify
those which present statistically significant differences
according to a pre-defined accuracy measure, and finally
cluster them into non-overlapping groups. The latter is a
desired property of the algorithm, since the traditional post-hoc
procedures produce overlapping groups causing difficulties in
the interpretation of the results [9]. Generally, the iterative
algorithm is consisted of consecutive steps, which aim at a
maximum differentiation between groups of models at each
stage. Each group can be partitioned again until no groups can
be split further.

The idea of evaluating ensembles is also a common subject
of a continuous research attracting the interest from several
scientific fields such as Machine Learning, Pattern
Recognition, Knowledge Discovery and Statistics.
Additionally, one of the authors of this study utilized the SK
test for combining classifiers on large datasets [15], where the


objective was the selection of the best subgroup among
different classification algorithms and the fusion of them
through a weighted voting schema.

Except from the scientifically grounded statistical
properties and notions that are integrated into a unified
methodology, the choice to utilize the approach of Mittas and
Angelis [9] is also stemmed from another practical advantage.
Despite the fact that project managers and practitioners are
willing to accept and evaluate well-defined statistical
methodologies, a significant obstacle towards this direction is
the lack of automated tools providing an easy-to-use manner
with which to perform such a meaningful and high-level
analysis. Indeed, the implementation of these statistical
techniques presupposes the involvement of an expert with
strong statistical experience but on the other hand, the findings
can easily be interpreted through automated tools.

Considering the practical necessity for the adoption of
automated tools in SCE, previous studies [10, 11] describe the
implementation of *Statistical Regression Error Characteristic*
*Curves* ( *StatREC* ), a statistical toolbox with a *Graphical User*
*Interface* (GUI) [12] that provides a friendly environment
contributing to the wise management of the SCE process.
Except from the traditional statistical hypothesis procedures
and other modern simulation resampling techniques, such as
bootstrap and permutation tests, StatREC also offers visual and
easily interpretable representation of many aspects of the
predictive power of estimation techniques.

factor.

V. C ONCLUSION

This paper investigates the effectiveness of using
ensembles for Web effort estimation. Two methodologies were
evaluated using the Tukutuku dataset of Web projects. Both
methodologies aim to build ensembles from individual


estimation techniques, chosen from a set of 90, that perform
the best on this dataset. The first approach is a replication of a
prior study that showed ensembles were better than individual
estimation techniques in the domain of general software
estimation. The second approach uses the SK algorithm to
identify the best cluster of effort estimation techniques with
which to build ensembles.


180

Authorized licensed use limited to: Aarhus University. Downloaded on May 23,2025 at 12:58:50 UTC from IEEE Xplore. Restrictions apply.


The results obtained from both approaches have been
promising. The 15 ensembles created in the first approach were
found to outperform all but two of the 90 solo techniques when
taking into account their maximal change in rank, �r, across the
three ranking systems used (wins, losses, and wins-losses). It
was also shown that these 15 ensembles made smaller errors
than their solo counterparts, avoiding the very large errors seen
with some solo techniques. Two ensembles were created by
the second approach and these ensembles, along with the 15
created in the first approach, were grouped in the best cluster of
effort estimation techniques by the SK algorithm. In other
words, despite the differences in methodology used to identify
the best solo techniques, ensembles of these best techniques
perform favourably in comparison to their solo counterparts,
based on the project data in the Tukutuku dataset.

This paper has focused on using the most accurate
estimation techniques with which to build ensembles. In the
field of machine learning this is only half the equation for
building effective ensembles; in addition to being accurate the
learners used to build an ensemble need to be diverse too [22,
23]. In other words, the individual learners used should not be
strongly correlated. They should make different errors on the
data. This has not been taken into account by either approach
used here. For example as seen in Table III, 12 out of the 16
solo estimation techniques used to build ensembles in the first
approach are variants of the CART algorithm. A useful future
direction would be to look at building ensembles from a
diverse set of accurate estimation techniques. Instead of simply
combining all the best solo estimation techniques, only the
ones that make different errors on the Tukutuku dataset should
be used. This will naturally necessitate having a deeper
understanding of the interdependencies between the different
estimation techniques with regards to the Tukutuku dataset.

Apart from combining different estimation techniques to
make an ensemble, another approach would be to combine
different instances of the same estimation technique. This can
be done by using different subsets of training data with a single
estimation technique [24]. This single estimation technique
would be chosen from the set of best techniques as identified
by either approach discussed in this paper. For example,
instead of using a leave-one-out cross-validation, as was the
case here, k-fold cross-validation could be used. Here the
dataset would be divided into *k* subsets, and by varying which
single subset is “left out”, *k* different but overlapping training
sets would be made each consisting of the remaining *k-1*
subsets [23]. Each training set would be used to make an
instance of a particular estimation technique (e.g. a particular
regression tree or neural network), and the estimates produced
by the *k* different instances would be combined to produce an
ensemble referred to as a “cross-validated committee” [22].

Different training sets can also be generated using a
resampling method known as bootstrap aggregation or bagging

[22, 23]. Using a training set of size *n*, bagging would generate
another training set by drawing *n* items from the original
training set with replacement. Each training set that is
generated by bagging is called a bootstrap replicate and
contains on average 63.2% of the original training set. Each
bootstrap replicate would be used to produce an instance of a


particular estimation technique which would then be combined
to form an ensemble.

By producing different training sets, cross-validation and
bagging reduce correlation when only one learner is used. They
work best on “unstable” learners; those that are sensitive to
small changes in training data [22]. This includes learners such
as CART; variants of this learner were identified as being part
of the best group of solo estimation techniques by our
replication and the SK algorithm. Another avenue of future
research would therefore be to investigate the effectiveness of
ensembles made from this learner using cross-validation and
bagging.

In addition to building on our work on ensembles from a
theoretical perspective, it is important to consider our work
from a practitioner’s point of view. In terms of performance it
does not really matter for a practitioner whether the best results
come from a solo learner or ensemble, but rather, which
particular technique provides the best estimates to be used later
for resource allocation. In that regard we have shown that
ensembles of the best solo techniques perform consistently
well. We do however have to ask whether this difference in
performance is sufficient enough to justify the extra time and
expertise required for their construction? The relevance of this
question is highlighted by the fact that both approaches
described in this paper identified solo techniques that
performed similarly to the ensembles. We feel that once we
have identified how best to construct ensembles, an evaluation
of that methodology in an industry setting from the viewpoint
of practitioners would provide a valuable validation.
