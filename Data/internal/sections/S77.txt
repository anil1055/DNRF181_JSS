***Abstract*** **—Even if the adaptation of the COBRA method to**
**the web has many appealing characteristics, its effectiveness**
**in predicting Web application development effort has been**
**previously assessed only in one empirical study, using the**
**Web Objects measure. The study provided positive results,**
**but it is widely recognized that several empirical**
**investigations should be performed to validate/confirm the**
**usefulness of effort estimation approaches. Moreover, the**
**use of other size measures in combination with Web-COBRA**

**has not yet been investigated in the literature. These two**
**aspects encouraged us to further analyze the effectiveness of**
**Web-COBRA. In particular, we used a dataset of 15 Web**
**applications, whose functional size was measured using**
**COSMIC. In this paper we describe the empirical analysis**
**we conducted, whose results confirm that Web-COBRA can**
**be profitably exploited for estimating Web application**
**development effort, also when used in combination with**
**COSMIC.**

I. I NTRODUCTION

In the field of Web Engineering various techniques
have been proposed to predict the effort required for the
development of an application [1], [3], [10], [18], [19],

[20], [21], [25], and among them Web-COBRA seems to
be of particular interest, thanks to its ability to combine
expert’s opinions with a formal modeling technique [25].
Web-COBRA is an adaptation of the COBRA method [4],
an acronym of COst estimation, Benchmarking and Risk
Analysis, since it provides an explicit support for these
three fundamental tasks of project management. COBRA
is a typical Composite Method, proposed to merge the pros
of both the Model and Non-Model Based methods [5], by
exploiting expert’s judgments, gathered in a controlled
fashion, with other cost drivers to be exploited with an
algorithmic approach. Indeed, Non-Model Based methods
are mainly based on expert’s guesstimates, while Model
Based methods rely on a more formal approach, involving
the application of some algorithms to a number of inputs
(i.e. factors that influence the development cost) to
produce an effort estimate. Among these factors, or “cost
drivers”, the size of the application is by far the most
widely used [5].

To the best of our knowledge, to date only one
empirical study has been carried out to assess the
effectiveness of Web-COBRA. In that work, the authors
employed Web Objects as size measure [23] and exploited
a dataset of 12 Web applications. The results obtained
highlighted the effectiveness of the method in the


considered context, but of course other replications in
different setting are required to assess the usefulness of the
proposal. Indeed, in the field of Empirical Software
Engineering, it is widely recognized that prediction
methods and size measures can be affordably applied in
industrial settings only if their effectiveness has been
empirically validated in different studies [27]. This fact
motivated us to conduct a new study on Web-COBRA.
Moreover, we were interested also in assess the
performance of the method in combination with COSMIC.
This because COSMIC is becoming one of the most
diffused methods for measuring the functional size of
software, while Web-COBRA allows us to take into
account also a number of factors that are relevant for a
specific context and can affect the development effort of
each specific project. For our empirical study, we exploited
a dataset composed of 15 Web applications developed by
an Italian software company.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In
Section II we describe the research method we exploited.
In Section III we report on the empirical analysis we
performed together with a discussion of the gathered
results. The validity of the empirical study is discussed in
Section IV while Section V contains a description of
related work. The paper ends by giving some final remarks
and suggestions on future work.

II. R ESEARCH METHOD

*A.* *The COBRA Method for the Web*

Web-COBRA was proposed to predict the
development effort of Web applications, taking into
account “the needs of a typical Web application
company”. In the following we describe the key aspects of
this method, but for sake of brevity it is not possible to
expose it in details. The interested reader can consult the
work in [25]. To apply Web-COBRA, two key aspects
have to be setup for a specific environment:

1. The set of external factors that can lead to a rise of

the cost for an application within the specific domain.
These factors are modeled by introducing the concept
of cost overhead, defined as “the additional
percentage on top of the cost of an application
running under optimal conditions” [4].

2. The relationship between cost overhead and
cost/effort.

In the following we detail these two points.
The first aspect is captured by a causal model, i.e. a list
of all the cost factors (and their relationships) that may
affect a development cost within a specific domain. This
conceptual, qualitative model is obtained through the
acquisition of experts’ knowledge. Then, the experts are
asked to “quantify” the effect of each of these identified
factors on the development cost, by specifying the
percentage of overhead above an “optimal” application
that each factor may induce. Since different experts may
provide different estimations of these percentages, based
on their previous experience, these factors are modeled as
uncertain variables requiring a minimal, most likely, and
maximal values. For example, experts may agree that the
factor “safety of the web application” may affect the
development cost/effort ranging from 10% (minimal),
through 50% (most likely), to 80% (maximal). To obtain
the causal model, a triangular distribution of these cost
overheads is calculated. It is worth noting that the range of
the distribution provides an indication on how uncertain
the experts are about the overhead inducted by the specific
cost factor [25].
In the second step, the relationship between the cost
overhead and the development cost is modeled by using
the Ordinary Least Squares Regression (OLSR) [22] and
employing past data of the company. The causal model
and the determined relationship between effort and cost
overhead are used to obtain the effort estimations for new
applications. In this step a Monte Carlo simulation can be
run to provide a distribution from where an estimate of the
effort can be obtained by taking the mean of the
distribution.

The main differences between COBRA and WebCOBRA regard the weighting of the cost factors. In
particular, OBRA allows to assign a weight to each expert
in order to take into account his/her degree of experience.
On the other hand, both in [25] and in this study, all the
company experts had very similar experience, making
unnecessary to “weight” their expertise.

*B.* *The Employed Size Measure*

COSMIC stands for COmmon Software Measurement
International Consortium and is a widely adopted method
for sizing software, approved as an ISO standard
(ISO/IEC 19761:2003). This method was originally
conceived for business and real-time applications, but it
can be successfully applied in many other contexts, where
the software is mainly dominated by large amounts of data
movements [9]. Indeed, the basic idea underlying the
COSMIC approach is that, for many kinds of software, the
main programming efforts are devoted to handle data
movements from/to the mass storage and the users. Thus,
their number can provide a meaningful sight of the system
size. In order to identify these data movements, a proper
context model for the specific application has to be
defined, bounding the software from its operating
environment. Basically each data movement crossing a
boundary has to be counted [9]. Four kinds of data
movements are considered: *Entry, Exit*, *Write*, *Read* . The
size of the software, expressed in terms of Cosmic


Functional Point [9], is given by the sum of all data

movements.
The application of the COSMIC method in the Web
domain has been analyzed by some researchers in the last
years [11], [18], [24]. The difficulties of applying the FPA
(Function Point Analysis) method for sizing an Internet
bank system, firstly motivated Rollo to use the COSMIC
in the context of Web applications [24]. However, he did
not present any empirical result supporting his thesis.
Subsequently, Mendes *et al.* applied the COSMIC
measurement to Web sites without server-side elaborations
and the context model they considered had the Web Server
as data store [18]. Using data about 37 Web systems
developed by academic students, they constructed an effort
estimation model by applying OLSR. Unfortunately, the
model did not provide good estimations. Subsequently, an
empirical study based on the use of 44 Web applications
(mainly Web portals, e-commerce sites, etc…), developed
by academic students, was performed to assess the
COSMIC approach [11]. The effort estimation model
obtained by employing OLSR provided encouraging
results. The analysis presented in the following takes into
account the suggestions of [11] for the application of the
COSMIC approach.

*C.* *Validation Technique and Evaluation Criteria*

As done in [25], we applied a leave-1-out cross
validation in order to verify whether or not the predicted
efforts were useful estimations of the actual development
efforts. It is widely used in empirical studies when dealing
with small datasets [5]. In particular, to apply the
technique, the original dataset is divided into N different
subsets (where N is the size of the original dataset) of
training and validation sets, where each validation set has
one observation. Then, N steps are performed and at each
step, the training set is used to determine the estimation
model and the validation set to assess the obtained

estimates.
To evaluate the accuracy of the obtained estimations,
we used some summary measures, namely MMRE,
MdMRE and Pred(0.25) [8], which have been widely used
in many comparisons to assess the accuracy of software
estimation models [10], [11], [18]-[21]. In the following,
we briefly recall the main concepts underlying the MMRE
and Pred(0.25). The Magnitude of Relative Error [8] is
defined as:

(1) MRE = |EFHreal — EFHpred | / EFHreal

where EFHreal and EFHpred are the actual and the
predicted efforts, respectively. MRE has to be calculated
for each observation in the dataset. All the MRE values are
aggregated across all the observations using the mean and
the median, giving rise to the Mean of MRE (MMRE), and
the Median MRE (MdMRE), where the latter is less
sensitive to extreme values [19].
The prediction at level 0.25 [8], defined as:

(2) Pred(0.25) = k /N

where k is the number of observations whose MRE is less
than or equal to 0.25, and N is the total number of
observations. Pred(0.25) is a quantification of the

percentage of predictions whose error is less than 25%.
According to [8], a good effort estimation model should
have a MMRE≤0.25 and Pred(0.25)≥0.75, meaning that
the mean estimation error should be less than 25%, and at
least 75% of the estimated values should fall within 25%

of their actual values.
As suggested in [20], we also analyzed MMRE,
MdMRE, and Pred(0.25) obtained by considering at each
step of the leave-1-out cross validation the mean of effort
(i.e., MeanEffort) and the median of effort (i.e.,
MedianEffort) across all the dataset of past projects, as
estimated value. The aim is to have a benchmark to assess

whether the estimates obtained with Web-COBRA and
COSMIC are significantly better than estimates based on
the mean and median effort of the previous projects.
Indeed, if this is not the case then, for a software company,
it could be more useful to simply use the mean or the
median effort of the past developed applications rather
than dealing with more complex estimation methods [20].
Moreover, we tested the statistical significance of the
obtained results, by using absolute residuals, in order to
establish if one estimation model provided better results
than others [16], [19]. In particular, we performed
statistical tests (the T-Test and the Wilcoxon test.) to verify
the following null hypothesis “the two considered
population have identical distributions”. If the null
hypothesis is true, then the number of positive and
negative differences should be approximately the same.
Furthermore, to have also an indication of the
practical/managerial significance of the results we verified
the effect size [14]. Effect size is a simple way of
quantifying the difference between two groups. It has
many advantages over the use of tests of statistical
significance alone since “whereas p-values reveal whether
a finding is statistically significant, effect size indicates
practical significance”. Employing the Wilcoxon test and
the T-test, the effect size was determined by using the
formula: r = Z-score / sqrt(N), where N is the number of
observations. In particular, we first calculated the effect
size and then compared it to the Cohen's benchmarks [7]:
so r=0.20 indicates a small effect, r=0.50 indicates medium
effect, and r=0.80 indicates a large effect.

*B.* *The Empirical Analysis*

To apply Web-COBRA, the following steps were
conducted:

1) Identification and quantification of cost factors.

2) Data collection for the Web applications.

Regarding the identification and quantification of cost
factors, a large number of cost drivers may affect the
development cost of software applications. However, for
each domain, only a subset of these factors results relevant

[4]. To select the set of the cost drivers that result
significant for the considered domain, we drafted an initial
list of cost drivers, based on a review of the literature on
Web effort/cost estimation, including the cost factors
identified in [25]. This preliminary list was submitted to
five experts of the software company involved in our
empirical study. Then a Delphi method [17] was adopted
until they agreed on the final set of cost drivers. They were
asked to comment, based on their experience, on the clarity

IV. C ASE STUDY VALIDITY

It is widely recognized that several factors can bias the
construct, internal, external, and conclusion validity of
empirical studies. To satisfy construct validity a study has
“to establish correct operational measures for the concepts
being studied” [15]. In other words, it represents to what
extent the predictor and response variables precisely
measure the concepts they claim to measure [19]. A
research study has internal validity if its outcome can be
considered as a function of the measured variables and not
of extraneous factors. Conclusion validity is concerned
with the ability to draw statistically correct conclusions
while external validity is concerned with the ability to
generalize the results to other contexts. In the following,
we will analyze the presented empirical study with respect
to each of these types of validity.
*Construct validity* relates to the choice of the size
measure and cost factors as well as the way to collect
them. Regarding the selection of the measure, we
employed a measure that has been used in the past to size
Web applications and estimate development effort. [11],

[18]. All the cost factors that may affect the development
effort of Web applications were identified together with
company experts, using a Delphi approach. To mitigate
biases, these factors are modeled as uncertain variables
requiring a minimal, most likely, and maximal values. The
collection of information about the measure, cost factors,
and actual effort represents the main difficulties to carry
out this kind of study.
Concerning the effort collection, the software company
used timesheets to keep track of this information, where
each team member annotates the information about his/her
development effort, and weekly each project manager
stored the sum of the efforts for the team. In order to
collect all the significant information to calculate the
values of the size measure, the authors defined a template
to be filled in by the project managers. All the project
managers were trained on the use of the questionnaires.
They took into account the COSMIC Measurement
Manual [9] and the rules provided in [11] for the collection
required to apply COSMIC.
To mitigate the threat to validity of the counting
results, one of the authors analyzed the filled templates and
the analysis and design documents, in order to cross-check
the provided information. The same author calculated the


values of the size measure. Moreover, the size of a Web
application was considered as an uncertain variable (by
using an uncertainty of 5% as done in [25]). Indeed
measuring the size of a system with a method not
experienced before may introduce some bias. As for the
collection of the information on the cost factors, we
defined a questionnaire together with some company
experts. Then, this questionnaire was submitted to the
project managers of the considered Web applications.
Some factors should be taken into account for the
*internal validity* : subjects’ authoring and designing
experience, and reliability of the data and lack of
standardization [3], [15], [18]. The subjects involved in the
study were professionals who worked in the software
company. No initial selection of the subjects was carried
out, so no bias was apparently introduced. Moreover, the
Web applications were developed with technologies and
methods that subjects had experienced. Consequently,
confounding effects from the employed methods and tools
can be excluded. As for the reliability of the data and lack
of standardization, the questionnaires used were the same
for all the Web applications and the project managers were
instructed on how to use the questionnaires, to correctly
provide the required information. Instrumentation effects
in general did not occur.
As for the *conclusion validity* we carefully applied the
statistical tests, verifying all the required assumptions.
However, the main threat to conclusion validity could be
related to the number of Web applications composing the
dataset. Indeed, 15 Web applications can be considered a
not so large number from a statistical point of view to
allow us to make generalization about the values obtained.
However, observe that Web-COBRA does not require a
large number of data to determine the relationship between
cost overhead an effort, which is the crucial step of the
method [4], [25].
The Web applications involved in this empirical
analysis are representative samples of modern Web
applications, taking into account their type, functionalities,
target platforms, and complexity. Thus, we are confident
that the type of analyzed Web applications did not bias the
validity of the achieved results.
Regarding generalization of the results, Web-COBRA
is a Composite Model Based method that uses both
experts’ knowledge and data about past applications to
define a company-specific estimation model, tailored for
an intended software development context. It is widely
recognized that the results obtained in an industrial context
might not hold in other contexts. Indeed, each context
might be characterized by some specific application and
human factors, such as development process, developer
experience, application domain, tools, technologies used,
time, and budget constraints [6]. Thus, replications of the
study in several and different companies are necessary to
generalize of the results.

In the previous sections we have analyzed the use of
Web-COBRA and COSMIC. In the following we discuss
related work taking into account empirical studies that
exploited other size measures and estimation methods.
The measure Web Objects [23] are characterized by the
use of four new Web-related components together with the

instances of the nine components are counted and a
complexity (low, average, or high) is associated to them.
Then, by using a calculation worksheet a weight is
associated to each counted instance of the components.
The total amount of Web Objects is obtained according to
the weights that are related to the complexity of each
component [23], giving an indication of the size of the
considered Web application. An empirical study based on
a dataset of 12 Web applications from industrial world was
performed to establish whether or not Web Objects can be
affordably used to estimate the development effort of Web
applications [25]. In particular, OLSR and Web-COBRA
were exploited to obtain estimates, gathering in both cases
encouraging results. More details about the empirical study
exploiting Web-COBRA have been provided in Section
III.C.
Some authors investigated the usefulness of size
measures such as number of Web pages, media elements,
internal links, etc. [1], [18], [19]. Several studies have been
conducted to investigate and compare the effectiveness of
these measures in combination with estimation techniques
like Linear and Stepwise Regression, Case-Based
Reasoning, Regression Tree, and Bayesian Networks [10],

[19], [21]. In [19] a dataset of 37 Web systems developed
by academic students was exploited and the empirical
results suggested that Stepwise Regression provided
statistically significant superior predictions than other
techniques when using length size measures, such as
number of Web pages, number of new media. On the
contrary, a study exploiting a dataset containing data on 15
Web software applications developed by a single Web
company (the ones also employed in the empirical study
presented in this paper) revealed that none of the employed
techniques (i.e., Stepwise Regression, Regression Tree,
and Case-Based Reasoning) was statistically significantly
superior than others [10].
Recently, Mendes and Mosley investigated the use of
Bayesian Networks (BN) for Web effort estimation using
the Web applications of the Tukutuku database [21]. They
employed two training sets, each with 130 Web
applications, to construct the models while their accuracy
was measured using two test sets, each containing data on
65 Web applications. They also compared the accuracy of
the obtained estimates with those obtained by exploiting
Manual Stepwise Regression and Case-Based Reasoning,
and median of effort and mean of effort. The analysis
revealed that Manual Stepwise Regression provided
significantly better estimations than any of the models
obtained by using Bayesian Networks and is the only
approach that provided significantly better results than the
median effort based model.
Baresi and Morasca proposed a different approach
defining several measures on the basis of attributes
obtained from design artifacts [3]. They observed that the
design phase is often a critical one and requires an
important part of the total effort of a Web application,
since the implementation can be partially automated by
tools that exploit design artifacts. In particular, they
reported on a case study, and two replications, conducted
with students of an advanced university class, that


highlighted the impact of some attributes, obtained from
artifacts designed with W2000, on the total effort required
for designing Web applications. It is worth noting that, in
our empirical study, Web applications were not
automatically obtained from design documents, thus our
focus was on the effort to develop Web applications.
Abrahão and Pastor proposed the OOmFPWeb method

[1] which maps the Functional Points concepts into the
primitives used in the conceptual modeling phase of
OOWS, a method for producing software for the Web.
More recently, Abrahão *et al* . have also proposed a
model-driven functional size measurement procedure,
namely the Object-Oriented Hypermedia Function Points
(OO-HFP), for Web applications developed using the
Object-Oriented Hypermedia (OO-H ) method [2]. This
proposal goes in the same line of Baresi and Morasca’s
proposal since the source code of Web applications can be
automatically generated from OO-H conceptual models
using a tool called VisualWade. An empirical validation of
this approach revealed that the obtained effort estimates
are comparable with those obtained by using the Tukutuku
measures [20].

VI. C ONCLUSIONS

In this paper, we have investigated the use of WebCOBRA proposed in [25] for estimating Web application
development effort. In particular, we have applied WebCOBRA in combination with the COSMIC measure
exploiting data from 15 industrial Web applications
developed by an Italian software company. The empirical
analysis confirms and extends the results provided in a
previous study since it reveals the effectiveness of WebCOBRA also in combination with another size measure,
namely COSMIC.
To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that
Web-COBRA is used with this size measure. The
COSMIC method has been developed to improve the
measurement of the functional size of a large number of
software types and has been recently used also for Web
applications [10], [11], [18], [24]. Thus, the empirical
study presented in this paper also corroborates the results
presented in [11]. In that paper, we showed the
effectiveness of COSMIC to be employed as size measure
for constructing effort estimation models for Web
applications using OLSR as modeling technique. The
combination of the Web-COBRA method with the
COSMIC measure appears to be especially interesting
since it allows us to combine functional aspects of a
software system (captured by COSMIC) with other factors
identified by the application of Web-COBRA.
In the future, we intend analyze other
extensions/adaptations of COBRA in the context of Web
applications, e,g., that provided in [26].
