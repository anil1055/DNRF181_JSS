**SUMMARY**

**Even though a significant number of estimation models have been proposed for development projects,**
**few have been proposed for software maintenance. This paper reports on two field studies carried out**
**on the use of functional size measures in building estimation models for sets of maintenance projects**
**implementing small functional enhancements in existing software. The first field-study reports on models**
**built with 15 projects making functional enhancements to an internet-based software program for linguistic**
**applications. The second field study analyses 19 maintenance projects on a single real-time embedded**
**software program in the defense industry. Both field studies collected functional size measures using version**
**2.0 of the COSMIC-FFP functional size measurement method. Also both field studies classified projects**
**into two classes of project difficulty in order to aid identifying subsets of projects with greater homogeneity**
**in the relationship of project effort to functional size. This paper is the first published paper reporting**
**on the use this second generation of functional size-measurement methods in a maintenance-estimation**
**context. Copyright**  **2002 John Wiley & Sons, Ltd.**

**1.** **INTRODUCTION**

Software maintenance is often defined as the modification of a software product after delivery to
correct faults, to improve performance or other attributes, or to adapt the product to a changed
environment [1,2]. Alternatively, maintenance is defined as work done on a software system after it
becomes operational [3,4]. Although software maintenance costs usually exceed software development
costs, Ferens indicates that the attention given to these costs has not been commensurate with their

importance, and, while a significant number of estimation models have been proposed for development
projects, few have been proposed for software maintenance [5].
Estimation models are based on the generic concept of productivity defined as the ratio of output
to input. In software projects, productivity is defined as the ratio of the software product developed to
the resources required to produce it. While ‘effort’ is the generally accepted measure of the input or
resources (often measured in person-hours, -days or -months), for output or product, software size is
recognized as a key factor in the models for estimating project effort [6]. In the work reported here, the
measurement concepts in functional size measurement methods were selected as the methodology for
defining a practical measure of the software maintenance work product from a functional perspective.
The goal of the field studies reported here is to investigate the development of estimation
models using as input the functional size of software enhancements, specifically as one of the key
parameters in modelling the relationship of functional size to maintenance work effort. This study
investigates, therefore, the following expected relationship or assumption between the functional size
of a maintenance project and the effort required to carry out the maintenance.

*The effort required to deliver a functional enhancement in a maintenance context increases*
*with the size of the enhancement (research question 1).*

In homogeneous environments, the above expected relationship or assumption can be easily
investigated with a minimum of variability of other factors. Of course, in most environments, there
is no such homogeneity, and some important factors will vary. Thus, the following assumption must
also be investigated.

*When the relationship between the increase in effort due to an increase in size is not strong,*
*some additional factors, in combination with functional size, contribute to the increase in*
*effort required (research question 2).*

To investigate these two research questions, two field studies were carried out in organizations which
had no prior measurement programs in place, and were using no formal estimation models for their
maintenance projects. In these field studies, no attempt was made to build generic estimation models
for all types of maintenance, but only for maintenance projects adding to or modifying the functionality
of an existing software application—i.e. enhancive maintenance [7].
Related work and the research methodology of the field studies are presented in Section 2. Both the
data and the estimation models from the first field study, on a Web-based application, are presented
in Section 3, and from the second study, on a real-time software application, in Section 4. Finally,
comparative results from these field studies are presented in Section 5, and concluding observations in
Section 6. Section 7 looks ahead with suggestions for future research. As has been done previously [8],
the full data set is available to encourage replication studies and for further investigation by interested
researchers [9,10].

**2.** **METHODOLOGY**

**2.2.** **Empirical investigation method: the field study**

Since little research has been conducted in the context of interest here—that is, estimation models
for multiple functional enhancements to the same software application—the empirical research
methodology of a field study was selected as the method to be used for this investigative work [14].
Lessons learned from such an investigation can then be used to refine further research questions and
to design experimental contexts for exploring them in more controlled environment and with a broader
empirical basis. This permits the generalization of research findings to contexts other than the ones
from which they were derived.
The empirical investigation work reported here is based on field studies carried out at two industrial
software organizations. While the research goals were the same for both organizations—i.e. the
construction of estimation models for the maintenance environment—the specific contexts were
different, as were the availability of data and the quality of data specific to each organization. The
development of the estimation models, and the analysis of their results, have to take these constraints
into consideration.

The two organizations were in the process of modifying or adding functions to existing real-time
software, respectively a Web-based application in the linguistic domain, and a real-time application
in the defense industry. Fifteen projects in one organization were measured during the process by
one of the staff members familiar with the organization and its software, while 21 projects were
measured after the fact by an outside observer in the other. The measurers had been trained by the same

This gives the average effort (in hours) to produce one unit of functional size. An example of a
productivity/estimation model built with a unit cost is *Effort* (estimated) = *Average cost* × *Size* .
The second type of model investigated is the *regression model* (both simple and multiple regression
models considered), with functional size units and other candidate factors as independent variables and
effort as the dependent variable.
The average unit cost estimation model was selected because it is easy to understand in an
industrial context: it provides an initial understanding of the general behaviour of a data set. The
regression estimation model was selected over more complex estimation models such as analogybased and neural-network models, which have not been proved to explain the size-effort relationship
in software projects any better [15,16]. Also simple regression models have been selected for
representing the estimation models in graphical form, over multiple regression models for which
graphical representation and interpretation are more challenging for practitioners.
In addition to size, it is recognized in the literature that other factors affect effort needed for doing
maintenance projects, such as type of application, programming language, age of software, and quality
of the documentation available. In the context reported here, the maintenance projects measured and
analyzed were carried out on the same software application, in the respective organizations; this means
therefore that these specific factors were held constant within these field studies and did not need to be
taken into account in building the estimation models within the same application under maintenance.
Still, many other factors can influence maintenance projects effort, in addition to size: for instance,
whether or not a full system test is required, severe constraints on resources availability, functional
complexity, technical complexity, low or high level of reuse, etc. In the field studies reported here,
these individual factors were not investigated independently, but were rather represented through a
single factor referred to as ‘project difficulty’ and expressed as a categorical variable.
Generally, factors such as ‘project difficulty’ are represented as categorical variables which are not
intended to represent quantitative values. Such categories are used to identify distinct groups of data
to be analyzed. The variables, by contrast, are used when categorical factors can affect the positive
relationship of variables in regression models. Therefore, they can be considered as explanatory
variables in estimation models [17].
To investigate research question 2, such a generic ‘project difficulty’ variable was collected in both
field studies to analyze whether or not such a variable could improve the estimation model previously

**2.4.** **Reliability criteria for estimation models**

Multiple statistical criteria exist for assessing whether or not a model reflects a data set, and its ability to
predict the behavior of the dependent variable. For example, the coefficient of determination *(R* [2] *)* and
the coefficient of variation give an indication of the quality of the model’s fit to the data. Pred(X) also
provides an assessment of the precision of the model. To assess the quality of the estimation models
to be built for use in an enhancive maintenance context, the following four statistical measures were
selected.

  - *Mean relative error (MRE)* . The MRE gives an indication of the divergence between the values
estimated by the model and the real values, as a percentage. Perfect estimates would give an
MRE of zero [18].

  - *Predictive quality of the model (PRED)* . In software engineering, an estimation model is
generally considered good when the MRE is within ±25% for 75% of the observations, or PRED
(0.25) = 0.75 [19].

  - *Coefficient of determination (R* [2] *)* . Another measure of the quality of a model is its coefficient
of determination *(R* [2] *)*, which describes the percentage of variability explained by the predictive
variable in the linear regression models. This coefficient has a value between 0 and 1; when an
*R* [2] is close to 1 it indicates that the variability in the response to the predictive variable can be
explained by the model, i.e. there is a strong relationship between the independent and dependent
variables.

  - *P value statistical parameter* . Still further, the coefficients of the independent variables in
estimation models are assessed using the *P* value statistical parameter, which expresses that
the statistical significance of the coefficient. Commonly, values lower than 0.05 are considered
statistically significant.

**2.5.** **Selected sizing method: COSMIC-FFP**

In the context of these field studies, functional size must be available as an input to the model, and it
must be a relevant and representative measure of size in this context. Quantifying the size of software
is generally recognized as one of the keys to adequate estimation of the effort, cost and scheduling of
software projects. The number of source lines of code (LOC) was the first generally accepted measure
for this purpose and is still used extensively, as demonstrated by the many estimation models that
include this measure as a key parameter, e.g. [12,20]. However, LOC can be reasonably determined
only fairly late in a project life cycle; on the other hand, functional size, determined on the basis of

functional user requirements, can be available much earlier for estimation purposes. Function Points
Analysis [21] now referred to as the IFPUG (International Function Points Users Group) method,
established the first generation of functional measure. It is still used today, but it, too, has some
limitations, which have been well documented over the past 15 years. One of these limitations is the
difficulty and relevance of applying such a method outside the MIS domain, as documented by, e.g.

[22–26].
In 1996, four major organizations doing business in Canada cooperated in the sponsorship of the
development of an extension for real-time and embedded software, which was initially placed in the
public domain under the name of Full Function Points [27]. Building on the strengths of this work
and with the support of the industry, the Common Software Measurement International Consortium
(COSMIC) was formed in 1998 to design and bring to market a new generation of functional size
measurement methods. The COSMIC group reviewed existing methods (IFPUG, MarkII [28], NESMA

[29] and version 1.0 of Full Function Points [27]), studied their commonalities and proposed the
basic principles on which the second generation of a software functional size measurement method
could be based [30]. In November of 1999, the group published version 2.0 of COSMIC-FFP [31], a
measurement method implementing these principles, and published its *Measurement Manual* on the
web for public access. Overall, close to 40 people from eight countries participated in the design of
this measurement method, and the *Measurement Manual* describing the method is available in English,
French, Japanese and Spanish.
This functional sizing method was designed to work equally well for ‘data-rich’ business/MIS
software and for ‘control-rich’ or ‘real-time’ software, as, for example, the software typically found in
telecoms, avionics and process control, and in embedded systems and operating systems. The method
also makes it possible to size such software in any layer or peer portion of a multi-layer and/or multitier architecture. The COSMIC-FFP measurement method explicitly defines a measurement system
which includes a measurement principle, base functional components, a standard unit of measure and
an aggregation function.

  - *Measurement principle* . Based on the COSMIC-FFP generic software model (Figure 1), the
method, through a defined set of rules and procedures, produces a numerical figure to represent
the functional size of this model. These rules implement the principle that the functional size of a
piece of software is directly proportional to the number of data movements it has. By convention,
this figure is then extended to represent the COSMIC-FFP functional size of the software itself.

  - *Base functional components* . Version 2.0 of the COSMIC-FFP measurement method uses only
four types of data-movements (Figure 2), referred to as base functional components in ISO/IEC
14143–1 [32]): entry, exit, read and write, all of which are clearly defined [31].

  - *Standard unit of measurement* . The standard unit of measurement, i.e. one COSMIC-FFP
functional size unit (1 *Cfsu* ), is defined by convention as equivalent to one single data movement
type at the sub-process level. Each of the COSMIC-FFP data movement types (e.g. entry, exit,
read and write) are assigned 1 *Cfsu* .

  - *Aggregation function* . The functional size of the data movement base functional components
can be combined to obtain the size of any higher-level functional structure, such as the size of
functional processes, of components within layers or of whole applications. This is achieved by
arithmetically adding the functional sizes of the constituent functional structures, in accordance
with the purpose of the measurement.

The measurement system proposed by the COSMIC-FFP measurement method offers a scalable
result, which means that the functional size figure can be constructed at any desired level of abstraction.
The COSMIC-FFP method is already in the process of being recognized as an international standard by
the ISO. In addition, the COSMIC-FFP method was designed with a much more granular scale, which
enables it to discriminate among the sizes of small functions—it uses a unitary size unit rather than the
stepwise functions of the first generation function measurement methods. Therefore, this COSMICFFP method opens up the possibility of building estimation models for real-time software based on
functional size, not only for development projects, but also for maintenance projects, including small
maintenance projects.
Although reports on the use of COSMIC-FFP in the construction of estimation models for
development projects have already been published [33–36], nothing has yet been published on its use
in maintenance contexts. This paper explores, through field studies in two organizations, the use of
this new generation of functional size measure (COSMIC-FFP) in building productivity and estimation
models for measuring the effort involved in small projects implementing functional enhancements in
the context of the maintenance of real-time software, including Web-based software.

**3.** **FIELD STUDY A**

**3.1.** **Context: industrial site A**

Organization A develops linguistic software for the Internet platform. It has a client–server architecture
and a multi-platform environment which allows text editors to use services from a local or distant
server. Its software is of the glue type, which acts as an entry point to online linguistic services.
It deals, for example, with translation engines, dictionaries and grammatical software tools. The
software is compatible, through the use of ‘plug-ins’, with other text software developed on Microsoft
Outlook [TM], Microsoft Word [TM], Microsoft Excel [TM], Microsoft PowerPoint [TM], Adobe FrameMaker [TM],
Adobe Illustrator [TM], Corel WordPerfect [TM], Eudora Light [TM], Netscape Communicator [TM], Netscape
Navigator [TM] and ICQ [TM] . The measurement and analysis work was carried out from April 1999 to
May 2000 [10].
In addition to functional size and effort, other factors which could have an impact on productivity
were collected for the analysis of the work–effort relationship, and categorical rating values assigned
using staff assessment of such variables. The Banker *et al* . [18] classification of candidate factors was
looked at for the selection of some additional factors. Values for these factors, for which there are
no international data collection standards, were selected by the staff working on these maintenance
projects. The objective was to investigate whether or not these additional variables contributed to
improving the estimation models, i.e. whether or not they made a positive contribution to the work–
effort relationship. Table I illustrates the definitions and ratings selected for two of these factors,
the experience of the staff working on the maintenance projects, and the difficulty of the projects
themselves.

the transformation was carried out by taking two dimensions into account simultaneously: the difficulty

However, in this field study both of these variables have a potential bias. On the one hand, it must
be taken into account that the difficulty levels were assigned by the staff members who completed
the project. Such ratings assignments are, of course, subjective, and, furthermore, there is a strong
possibility that such an assignment might have been affected by the experience of the staff. For
example, a project classified as ‘highly difficult’ by a beginner might have been rated as either of
low or medium difficulty by a more experienced staff member. On the other hand, while the variable
experience of staff is, by itself, not directly related to the difficulty of the maintenance project, the
assignment of a maintenance project to a specific staff member often has a definite management bias.
For example, difficult projects may be assigned to more experienced maintainers, and simple projects
to less experienced maintainers. There are, of course, variations due to scheduling constraints and to
training opportunities (time permitting, of course). In this field study, it could be postulated that simple
projects were, in general, assigned to less experienced staff, and the most difficult ones to the most
experienced staff, with variations for projects of medium difficulty. Consequently, there is a possibility
that the difficulty variable used up to now has some structural bias, and is not truly representative of
the inherent difficulty of the project itself.

variable (measured in person-hours to complete a functional enhancement project) with the variation
in the functional size of such a project, and from there to infer the maintenance cost of a modification
to the software of the system. Two types of estimation models are used to investigate the variation.

**3.3.** **Average unit cost models**

*3.3.1.* *With functional size only*

The unit cost model was calculated on this sample of 15 projects carried out within organization A:
1.47 hours */Cfsu* . The estimation model built from this average unit cost is then: *effort* = 1 *.* 47 × *Cfsu* .
The MRE of this unit cost model is 81.28%, which is much higher than the recommended 25% [19];
only three projects (20% of the sample) have an estimated value within 25% of the actual effort.

**4.** **FIELD STUDY B**

**4.1.** **Context: industrial site B**

Data set B comes from a for-profit organization which designs, develops and implements systems

**5.** **ANALYSIS AND RESULTS**

The summary in Table XII facilitates a comparison of the data sets and of the estimation models from
the two field studies. The two data sets are similar in some respects, and are also significantly different
in others. The following comparisons do not include the two outliers excluded from the analysis of the
data set from organization B.
In terms of similarities, both data sets comprise maintenance projects modifying a single software
application. The functional size has been measured with the COSMIC-FFP standard, by measurers
trained by the same trainer. Both data sets also lie in the same range, in terms of project effort and
functional size. Also, neither organization had a measurement program in place, neither had developed
their own estimation models (either for development or for maintenance projects).
Presenting the two data sets as scatter diagrams with functional size on the horizontal axis and effort
in person-hours on the vertical axis, shows positive relationship between size and effort. But it also
shows that the relationship is not strong. Both scatters have a wedge shape, which indicates that, for
these organizations, at least one other factor has a significant influence on project effort.
In terms of differences, the average size of the maintenance projects in the Web-based
organization (A) is four times the average size of the maintenance projects in the defense
organization (B), but requires on average only twice as much effort; this leads to an average unit cost
for organization A which is only half that for organization B.
For both data sets, most of the estimation models investigated yielded poor estimation models, either
on the functional size variable or on the other categorical variables investigated, such as staff experience
and project difficulty. This applies to the average unit cost models, the simple linear regression models
and the nonlinear regression models.
However, when both functional size and a two-level difficulty classification of projects are taken
into account in a multiplicative regression model, then the performance of such models improves
significantly. In particular, for organization B, projects of high difficulty have a model on size with

projects consisting of an unusually high level of reuse, might have a relationship between size and
work effort which is much smaller than other projects involving almost no reuse.

**6.** **CONCLUDING OBSERVATIONS**

The goal of this study was to investigate the development of estimation models for maintenance
projects, using as input the functional size of software enhancements as one of the key parameters
for modeling the relationship between the size of a maintenance project and the maintenance work
effort. In this study, we therefore investigated the following expected relationship.

*The effort required to deliver a functional enhancement in a maintenance context increases*
*with the size of the enhancement (research question 1).*

In a homogeneous environment, the above assumption might be investigated, and determined
through observation to be true, provided that other factors vary minimally at most. However, in most
environments, there is no such homogeneity, and some important factors will vary. Thus, the following
assumption was also investigated.

*When the relationship between the increase in effort due to an increase in size is not strong,*
*some additional factors, in combination with functional size, contribute to the increase in*
*effort required (research question 2).*

Software size is recognized as a key factor in the construction of models to estimate project effort.
Software size can be measured from either a technical perspective, with LOC for example, or from a
functional perspective. While functional size measures have been available for software of the MIS type
for the past 20 years, the industry has not yet felt that it was appropriate for real-time and embedded
software. The COSMIC-FFP new generation of functional size measures has addressed this domain
issue. In addition, the COSMIC-FFP method was designed with a much more granular scale, that
enables it to discriminate among the various sizes of small functions through the use of a unitary size
unit rather than through the use of stepwise functions as in the first-generation measurement method.
This COSMIC-FFP method thus makes it possible to build estimation models for real-time software

based on functional size, and to do this not only for development projects, but for maintenance projects,
both large and small, as well.
This paper has reported on two field studies carried out to investigate these research questions in the
context of small enhancive maintenance projects. The field studies were carried out in organizations
which had no maintenance measurement programs in place, and no formal estimation models for their
maintenance projects. Both software organizations would also be considered to be in the initial stages
of process maturity. The two data sets shared a common characteristic: they were a set of maintenance
projects adding to or modifying functions in a single existing application. In terms of descriptive
statistics, the average project effort for the two organizations was respectively 115 person-hours and
221 person-hours.
Estimation models built with either an average unit cost or a simple regression model provided
interesting models for both organizations, but they did not model the relationship between size and
effort well for the individual projects. For the data sets of the two field studies reported here, even
though there is a clear positive relationship between functional size and project effort, such a relation
was not strong enough to derive good estimation models using this single independent variable with
either the average unit costs models or the linear and nonlinear forms of simple regression models. Both
data sets had wedge-shaped scatter diagrams, illustrating that the single size variable is not enough for
building a good estimation model. Hence, the first research question did not lead to estimation models
with a satisfactory performance at the individual project level.
For the investigation of the second research question, we looked at the contribution of other
categorical variables such as staff experience and project difficulty. Various estimation models were
developed based on these variables, either using average unit costs, linear regression or nonlinear
regression on full samples, or dividing the samples into subsets according to categorical criteria.
In particular, a project difficulty variable was used and investigated further. Such a project difficulty
variable was determined by subject matter experts in each organization, and in accordance with their
own culture and understanding. Such a variable is a categorical variable; it can be used to classify the
data points of a sample into specific subsamples which are more homogeneous with respect to their
own categorical classification.
The two field studies have investigated the impact of such a categorical variable in improving the
performance of estimation models that already take into account the functional size of the maintenance
projects. A three-level difficulty classification was initially selected in one field study, while a four-level
classification was selected in the other. However, for the size of the samples available, there were not
enough data points, and in both field studies the levels had to be transformed into a simpler two-level
difficulty classification using empirical procedures.
Two forms of regression model were then investigated: additive models and multiplicative multiple
regression models. While regular multiple regression gives a single model, both the additive and the
multiplicative models lead to two models, one for each class of the second independent variable. In the
additive model, the two submodels are represented by two parallel lines, with the same slope and a
different point of origin; in the multiplicative model, the two submodels do not need to be parallel
lines. In both field studies, the multiplicative models graphically mapped the two classes of projects
much better (low–high) with respect to the other independent variable, functional size, and their quality
as good models of the relationship between size and work effort were much better.
These results are of particular interest in the development of estimation models for maintenance
projects: the functional size of a maintenance enhancement can be measured very early on from

the requirements themselves, and therefore this size can be obtained fairly accurately before any

As seen in these field studies, the introduction of a difficulty variable as a second independent factor
in multiplicative regression models has improved the estimation results for the individual maintenance
projects significantly. Readers are reminded that such a variable needs to be estimated early on in
the project, thereby introducing a certain level of uncertainty as to whether or not a project would be
appropriately classified.
With these two field studies, the various analyses have provided insights into maintenance work,
which consists of small functional enhancements and their relationships with work effort. A process
for deriving estimation models has also been illustrated, with both an objectively derived quantitative
variable (functional size), obtainable early on in the project life cycle, and a categorical variable,
difficulty. Once such models are derived, and their quality analyzed, then they can be quite useful in
estimating subsequent maintenance projects involving adding or modifying functions to these software
applications.

**7.** **FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS**

The limitations of the two field studies were defined by the empirical research methodology used and
the constraints of the data sets available. Within each organization, all the data came from functional
enhancements to the same software application, and therefore no investigation was carried out to
explore how such results could be applied to enhancements to other software applications, within the
same organization, or across functional domains. Also, the projects in each organization were of the
same type, that is, either Web-based linguistic glue software or real-time embedded software. With no
data available from other organizations of either type, it would not be prudent to extrapolate results to
other organizations or for other types of software; however, the analytical process itself can be reused
for building such models. Access to much larger data sets, and from many other types of real-time
software, would make it possible to investigate whether or not these results could be generalized to
larger contexts. Finally, further work is required to understand better the ‘project difficulty’ generic
variable used to represent other factors of influence and to define better their categorization to avoid
the transformations used in these field studies.
