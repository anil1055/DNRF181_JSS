**Abstract**

*Software practitioners recognise the importance of*
*realistic estimates of effort to the successful management*
*of software projects, the Web being no exception. Having*
*realistic estimates at an early stage in a project's life*
*cycle allow project managers and development*
*organisations to manage resources effectively.*

*Prediction is a necessary part of an effective process,*
*be it authoring, design, testing, or Web development as a*
*whole.*

*The first part of this paper describes a case study*
*evaluation (CSE) where we measured characteristics of*
*Web applications and the effort involved in designing and*
*authoring those applications. The second half presents*
*two prediction models generated using statistical*
*techniques, namely linear regression and stepwise*
*multiple regression. The prediction power of the models*
*employed is then compared using boxplots of the*
*residuals. Results suggest that stepwise regression gives*
*better predictions than linear regression.*

A prediction process involves:

- Identification of size metrics, cost drivers and the

- Generation of prediction models to be applied to

- Assessment of the effectiveness of those prediction

This paper focuses on effort prediction for design and
authoring processes. We adopt the classification proposed
by Lowe and Hall [2] where authoring encompasses the

include aspects such as hypermedia application

applications maintenance. In addition, our design phase
also incorporates the conceptual design of the application,
reflected in map diagrams showing documents and links.

The data used to generate our prediction models were

effort prediction were measured. A number of
complexity metrics from literature in Software
Engineering [3] and Multimedia [4, 5] were reused. In
addition, several media metrics were adapted from [6].
The Prediction models proposed were generated by

**2.** **Background**

The original motivation for deriving and using
measurement was provided by managers who wanted to
predict project costs during early phases in the software
life-cycle. As a result several models for cost and effort
estimation have been proposed and used [3].

Existing approaches to effort prediction fall into three
general categories [71, as follows:

I) Expert judgement
2) Algorithmic models
3) Analogy

I) Expert judgement has been a method widely used
over the years. However, the means of deriving an
estimate are not explicit and therefore not repeatable.
Expert opinion, although always difficult to quantify,
can be an effective estimating tool on its own or as an
adjusting factor for algorithmic models [8]. .
2) Algorithmic models, to date the most popular Ill the
literature, attempt to represent the relationship
between effort and one or more project
characteristics. The main "cost driver" used in such a
model is usually taken to be some notion of the
software size (e.g. the number of lines of source
code, number of pages, number of links).
Algorithmic models always need to be calibrated or
adjusted to local circumstances. Examples of
algorithmic models are the COCOMO model [9], the
SLIM model [1OJ and function points [11].
3) Estimation by analogy involves characterising the
project for which an estimate is required and use this
characterisation as a basis for finding similar or
analogous completed projects, for which effort is
known. These effort values are then used, possibly
**with** adjustment, to generate a predicted value of
effort. Finding the right analogies and assessing the
degree of similarity are the principal difficulties of
this method.
An advantage of algorithmic models over estimation
and expert judgement is to allow users to see how a model
derives its conclusions, an important factor for
verification as well as theory building and understanding
of the process being modelled [12]. However these
models need to be calibrated relative to the local
environment in which they are used, considered by some
to be a disadvantage.

Recently several comparisons have been made
between the three categories of prediction models
presented in this Section, based on their prediction power

[12,13,14,15,16]. However no convergence has been
obtained to date.

**3.1** **Introduction**

The case study evaluation measured several attributes
(metrics) of Web applications and the effort involved in
Web design and authoring. The attributes reflect our
notion of product size, complexity, characteristics of the
application, process and resources expected to influence
effort in some way.

Our measurement activity was developed according to
Fenton and Pfleeger's Conceptual Framework for
Software Measurement [3]. This framework can be
applied to the diverse software-measurement activ_ities
that contribute to an organisation's software practices.
The practices can be development, maintenance,
experiments and case studies performed to investigate
new techniques and tools. The frmnework is based on
three principles:

I. Classifying the *entities* to be examined.
2. Determining relevant *measurement goals.*
3. Identifying the *level of maturity* the *organisation* has
reached.

Each of these principles is described in the next sub-­
sections.

"entities required by a process activity" [3]. . .

**3.1.3** **The Level of Maturity.** The Capability Maturity
Model (CMM) was designed to guide software
organisations in selecting process improvement strategies
by "determining current process maturity and identifying
the few issues most critical to software quality and
process improvement" [18]. It provides a framework for
organising the evolutionary steps into five maturity levels
that lay successive foundations to continuous process
improvement. These five maturity levels define an ordinal
scale for measuring the\ maturity of an organisation's
software process and for evaluating its software process
capability. The levels also help an organisation prioritise
its improvement efforts. Each maturity level comprises a
set of process goals that, when satisfied, stabilise an
important component of the software process [18].

The levels of maturity within the Web application
development community considered for our case study
are either level l or 2.

**3.4** **Validity of the Case Study**

Comments on the validity of the case study are as
follows:
#### • Design and authoring effort are difficult concepts to

measure. However, it is argued that the data collected
and the response variable derived are intuitively
reasonable measures.

- There is no standard on what constitutes the design
and authoring processes of a Web application.
Therefore, the activities used in this study may not be
representative of all practices employed in the Web
community.

- The metrics collected, apart :from effort, experience
and structure, were all objective and precisely
quantifiable. The scale used to measure experience
and the three types of structure of an application were
described in detail in both questionnaires.
#### • There were four confounding factors in the case

study evaluation:

l. Subjects' authoring and designing experience.
2. Maturation effects, i.e. learning effects caused by
subjects learning as an experiment proceeds.
3. Structure of the application.
4. Tools used to help author and design the Web
application.

The data collected revealed that:

    - Subjects' authoring and design experiences
were mostly scaled little (experience=2) or
average (experience=3), with a low skill
differential 6. A scatterplot between
experience and effort was used to
investigate their relationship. Most datapoints
fell within clusters between the interval 10 to
80 hours, either for experience 2 or 3.
Consequently the original dataset was left
intact.

   - Subjects had to develop a Web application as
part of their first assignment. They also
received training in the CFT principles,
reducing maturation effects.

   - Notepad (or similar text editor) and FirstPage
were the two tools most frequently used.

6 low difference between skill levels


Notepad is a simple text editor while
FirstPage is freeware offering button-­
embedded HTML tags. Although they differ
with respect to the functionality offered, a
scatterplot between total-effort and tool
revealed that for both tools most datapoints
fell within the interval between IO to 80
hours. Consequently, confounding effects
from the tools were reduced.
#### • The selection effects, which are due to natural

variations in subject performanc:f:, were controlled.
#### • The instrumentation effects in general did not occur

in this evaluation; the questiormaires used were the

same.

- The results may be domain dependent as all subjects
answered the questionnaires based on their
experience in developing Web applications for
education. This evaluation should therefore be
repeated in domains other than education if the
results are to be generalised to other domains.
#### • The applications did not have more than l 03

documents (mean=57) and 1501 links (mean 616).
These numbers represent medium size applications,
according to [2]. Consequently they are not
representative of the large Web applications for
education developed by some organisations.
However, the applications developed had similar to
or even better interface and contents quality than
Web applications developed by professionals (as
observed by one of the authors when marking the

- A threat to subject generalizability [22] may exist
when the subject population is not drawn :from a
wider population. The subjects that participated in the
case study were either final year undergraduate
students or MSc students. It is likely that they present
skill sets similar to Web professionals at the start of
their careers. The use of students as subjects, while
sometimes considered unrealistic, is justified for two
reasons: firstly, empirical evidence by Bohem-Davis
and Ross [23] indicates that students are equal to
professionals in many quantifiable measures,
including their approach to developing software;
secondly, for pragmatic considerations, having
students as subjects was the only viable option for

**4.1** **Introduction**

For the collected data, linear regression and stepwise
regression were used to generate effort prediction models
for Web design and authoring.

The purpose of linear and stepwise multiple regression
models are to learn more about the relationship between
several predictor variables and a response variable.
Multiple regression and linear regression allow the
researcher to ask (and hopefully answer) the general
question "what is the best predictor of ..." [24).

Regression modelling is one of the most widely used
statistical modelling techniques for fitting a quantitative
response variable *y* as a function of one or more predictor
variables *X1,* *x* *2, ...* *,xp.* Regression models are widely used
as they often provide an excellent fit to a response
variable when the true functional relationship between the
response and the predictors is unknown **[8].**

Both linear regression and stepwise regression were
calculated using SPSS 10.0.5.

**4.3** **Comparing the Prediction Models**

We used boxplots of the residuals to compare the two
prediction models generated, as suggested by Pickard et
al. [25] and Kitchenham et al. [26]. It gives a good


indication of the distribution of the residuals and can help
explain the behaviour of the summary statistics.

A good summary description of boxplots is given in
Kitchenham et al. [26]:

"Boxplots are based on non-parametric statistics. They
show the median value as the central tendency for the
distribution. If the median is close to one, the
predictions are unbiased. The length of the box from
lower tail to upper tail gives an indication of the spread
of the distribution. The position of the median in the
box and the legth of the boxplot tails show the skewness
of the distribution. If the upper and lower tails are
approximately equal and the median is in the centre of
the box the distribution is symmetric. If the distribution
is not symmetric the relative lengths of the tails and the
position of the median in the box indicate the nature of
the skewness. The lenght of the box relative to the
length of the tails gives an indication of the shape of the
distribution. A boxplot with a small box and long tails
represents a very peaked distribution, a boxplot with a
long box represents a flatter distribution."

Boxplots offer a simple way of comparing predictions
from different prediction systems. The boxplots for the
residuals (difference between the actual and predicted
values for effort) of the two prediction models generated

**5.** **Conclusions and Future Work**

This paper has presented a case study evaluation in
which we measured attributes to be used on effort

predictions. The datasets obtained represent attributes of
several entities, namely Web application, Web design and
authoring processes, Developer and Authoring tool.
Although some of the suggested metrics can be used in
the prediction of effort for dynamically generated Web
applications, the metrics were mostly related to static
applications.

In addition, the paper proposes effort prediction
models generated using two statistical techniques, namely
Linear regression and Stepwise Multiple Regression.

Although the prediction models generated presented
biased predictions we are aware that other techniques,
such as General Linear Models (GLMs), have the
advantage of allowing relationships between response and
predictor variables to be non-linear, provide considerable
flexibility while still yielding to a rigorous statistical
treatment. In addition, there are also other non-­
algorithmic techniques which, in the same way as linear
models, allow the user to see how a model derives its
conclusions [12]. These techniques are fuzzy systems and
case-based reasoning. The use of GLMs and case-based
reasoning represent the scope of our future work.

We also plan to apply effort prediction to the whole
development cycle of Web applications and to compare
the performance of several prediction techniques against
human estimation.

To conclude, there is an urgent need for adequate Web
development effort prediction at an early stage in the
development. As the use of the Web as a resource
delivery environment increases, effort estimation can
contribute significantly to the reduction of costs and time
involved in developing Web applications.
