**Abstract**

*Analogy-based estimation has, over the last 15 years,*
*and particularly over the last 7 years, emerged as a*
*promising approach with comparable accuracy to, or*
*better than, algorithmic methods. In addition, it is*
*potentially easier to both understand and apply; these*
*two important factors can contribute to the successful*
*adoption of estimation methods within Web development*
*Companies* *We* *believe* *therefore,* *analogy-based*
*estimation should be examined further.*
*This paper replicates previous work that investigated*
*the use of two types of adaptation rules as a contributing*
*factor to better estimation accuracy. In addition, it also*
*investigates the use of Feature Subset Selection, in*
*addition to Adaptation rules. Two datasets are used in the*
*analysis; results show that adaptation rules improved*
*estimation accuracy for the less “messy” dataset. Feature*
*Subset Selection also seems to help improve the*
*adaptation results.*

**1.** **Introduction**

Most approaches to software cost estimation have
focused on Expert opinion and algorithmic models.
Expert opinion is widely used in industry, despite the fact
that the means of deriving an estimate are not explicit and
are therefore difficult to repeat. Expert opinion, although
difficult to quantify, can be an effective estimation tool on
its own or as an adjusting factor for algorithmic models

[22].
Algorithmic models attempt to represent the
relationship between effort and one or more project


features, where the main feature is usually taken to be
some notion of software size (e.g. the number of lines of
source code, number of Web pages, or number of
graphics). These models require calibration to local
circumstances and can be discouraging to adapt and use
where there is limited or incomplete data, and limited
expertise in statistical techniques. Examples of such
models are the COCOMO model [2] and the SLIM model

[19].
Analogy-based estimation has, over the last 15 years,
and particularly over the last 7 years, emerged as a
promising approach with comparable accuracy to, or
better than, algorithmic methods. In addition, it is both
potentially easier to understand and apply; two important
factors to the successful adoption of estimation methods
within Web development Companies. We believe
therefore, analogy-based estimation should be examined
further.

This paper replicates previous work that investigated
the use of adaptation rules as a contributing factor to
better estimation accuracy [11]. Replicated studies allow
researchers to merge knowledge, directly or via metaanalysis [13]. As in other Sciences, replication is
fundamental to establishing the validity and
generalisability of results [20]. Mendes et al. [11]
compared two types of adaptation rules for a number of
methods of analogy-based effort estimation. Two datasets
presenting very different characteristics (outliers,
collinearity) were used in the analysis; One of these
datasets presented a continuous “cost” function, translated
as a strong linear relationship between size and effort, and
was less “messy” [21], reflected by the absence of outliers
and small collinearity; the other dataset presented a

**2.** **Related Work**

This Section presents previous work where authors
have employed CBR using adaptation techniques to build
prediction systems for Web cost estimation. Readers
interested in the use of CBR in Software Engineering are
referred to [4] for an overview.
Mendes and Mosley [7] **(1** **[st]** **study)** investigate the use
of CBR for Web hypermedia effort prediction by
comparing the prediction accuracy of eight CBR
techniques, of which three have previously been compared
in [8]. Some of those techniques employed one of the
types of adaptation used in this paper, named “adaptation
without weights”. They also compare the prediction


accuracy of the best CBR technique against stepwise
regression, using a twenty-fold cross-validation. All
prediction accuracies were measured using Mean
Magnitude of Relative Error (MMRE), Median Magnitude
of Relative Error, Prediction at level l (l=25%), and
boxplots of the residuals. Their results were based on data
on Web projects obtained via a case study evaluation [9].
Mendes et al. [11] **(2** **[nd]** **study)** investigate further the
use of adaptation rules as a contributing factor to better
estimation accuracy. They employ two types of
adaptations, “adaptation without weights” and adaptation
using weights”. These two types of adaptation will be
described in Section 3. Two datasets on Web projects are
used in the analysis; results show that the best predictions
are obtained for the dataset that first, presents a
continuous “cost” function, translated as a strong linear
relationship between size and effort, and second, is less
“messy” in terms of outliers and collinearity. The
adaptation rule that did not use weights produced the best
results. Prediction accuracy was measured using MMRE,
Pred(25) and boxplots of absolute residuals.
This paper replicates the 2 [nd] study using industrial
Web project datasets, part of the Tukutuku database [10].
However they differ in that one (DS1) has data on 12
Web projects developed by the same Web company
whereas the other has data on 37 Web projects developed
by several Web companies.
Results are compared using MMRE and Pred(25) as
measures of prediction accuracy. MMRE was chosen for
two reasons: First, MMRE is the de facto standard
evaluation criterion to assess the accuracy of software
prediction models [18]; Second, recent empirical
investigation did not find evidence to discourage the use
of MRE and MMRE [12]. Prediction at level *l*, also
known as Pred( *l* ), is another indicator which is commonly
used. It measures the percentage of estimates that are
within *l* % of the actual values.
The statistical significance of the results was based on
absolute residuals, tested using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum
Test, since the data was naturally paired. The confidence
limit was set at �=0.05. Non-parametric tests were
employed since the absolute residuals for all the models
used in this study were not normally distributed, as
confirmed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for nonnormality.

**3.1** **Feature Subset Selection**

Feature subset selection involves determining the
optimum subset of features that give the most accurate
estimation. Some existing CBR tools, e.g. ArchANGEL,
offer this functionality optionally by applying several
techniques such as exhaustive search, forward sequential
selection, hill-climbing [5]. In the context of this
investigation, as we had more than 20 features. We
obtained the best feature subset via Forward sequential
selection, as suggested in [5].

**3.2** **Similarity Measure**

Similarity Measure measures the level of similarity
between cases. Several similarity measures have been
proposed in the software engineering literature; however
the ones described herein and used by this study are the
unweighed Euclidean distance, the weighted Euclidean
distance and the Maximum distance [11]. Readers are
referred to [17] for details on other similarity measures.
Each similarity measure we used is described below:

**3.2.1** **Unweighted** **Euclidean** **Distance.** The
Unweighted Euclidean distance measures the Euclidean
(straight-line) distance between two points. This measure
has a geometrical meaning as the distance of two points in
the n-dimensional Euclidean space [17].

**3.2.2** **Weighted Euclidean Distance.** The weighted
Euclidean distance is used when features vectors are

given weights that reflect the relative importance of each
feature. Weight was calculated using the two approaches
employed in [11]:
1. On both datasets we attributed weight=2 to size
measures Webpages, ImgNew and Tot-high and
weight =1 to the remaining measures. The choice of
weights was the same used in [11] and also reflected
existing literature [1,10].
2. We measured the linear association between the

predictors and response variable using a two-tailed
Spearman’s rho test, a non-parametric test equivalent
to Pearson’s test, for both datasets. The choice of test
was based on the characteristics of the measures

employed. As measures did not present a normal
distribution of values or very close to normal, we used
a nonparametric test. For both datasets, some
correlations presented negative values. Given that
CBR-works does not accept negative values as
weights, we added +1.0 to all correlations, making all
values positive and thus suitable for use. All the
results presented in this paper have taken the +1 effect
into account.

**3.2.3** **Maximum Distance.** The maximum measure

computes the highest feature similarity, defining the most
similar project. This distance effectively reduces the
similarity measure down to a single feature, although the
maximum feature may differ for each retrieval
occurrence. In other words, although we used several size
measures, for a given “new” project p, the closest project
in the case base will be the one with a size measure that

has the most similar value to the same measure for that

project *p* .

**3.3** **Scaling**

Scaling or Standardisation represents the
transformation of attribute values according to a defined
rule such that all attributes have the same degree of
influence and the method is immune to the choice of units

[17]. Similarly to [11], we have scaled all features used in

**3.4** **Number of Cases**

The number of cases refers to the number of most

similar projects that will be used to generate the
estimation. As we were replicating previous work, we
employed the same number of cases as [11], therefore we
used 1, 2 and 3 most similar cases.

**3.5** **Case Adaptation**

When most similar case(s) has/have been selected, we
next decide how to generate the estimation for the “new”
project *p* . In the context of this work we use the mean,
median and the inverse rank weighted mean, similarly to

[11]. Each adaptation is explained below:
�� Mean: Represents the average of k cases, where k>1.
The mean is a typical measure of central tendency
used in the Software/Web engineering literature. It
treats all cases as being equally influential on the

outcome.

�� Median: Represents the median of k cases, where
k>2. Another measure of central tendency and a more
robust statistic when the number of most similar

cases increases [17].
�� Inverse rank weighted mean: Allows higher ranked cases to
have more influence than lower ones. If we use 3 cases, for
example, the closest case (C) would have weight = 3, the
second closest (S) weight = 2 and the last one (L) weight
=1. The estimation would then be calculated as (3*C + 2*S
+ L)/6).

**3.6** **Adaptation Rules**

Adaptation rules are often used to reflect feature
differences between the new problem and the retrieved
cases [4]. In our context, rules are used to adapt the
estimated effort, according to a given criterion, such that
it reflects the characteristics of the target project more
closely. For example, in the context of effort prediction,
the estimated effort to develop an application “ *app”*
would be adapted such that it would also take into
consideration an *app* ’s size values and any other cost
drivers.

The adaptation rules employed in [11] and in this
study are based on the linear size adjustment to the
estimated effort [14].


For Walkerden and Jeffery [14], once the most similar
finished project in the case base has been retrieved, its
effort value is adjusted to estimate effort for the target
project. A linear extrapolation is performed along the
dimension of a single measure, which is a size measure
strongly correlated with effort. The linear size adjustment
is represented as follows:

**5.** **Comparing Adaptation Techniques**

To compare the Adaptation techniques we used the
jack-knife method (also known as leave one out crossvalidation). It is a useful mechanism for validating the
error of the prediction procedure employed [17]. We
repeated the method 12 times for DS1 (12 cycles) and 37
times for DS2 (37 cycles)., as we had 12 and 37 projects
respectively [11]. All projects in both datasets were
completed projects for which actual effort was known.
**Step 1:** Project number *i* (where *i* varies from 1 to 12/37)
is removed from the case base such that it is

considered a new project for the purpose of the
estimation procedure.
**Step 2:** The remaining 11/36 projects are kept in the case
base and used for the estimation process.
**Step 3:** The CBR tool finds the most similar cases,
looking for projects that have feature values
similar to the feature values for project *i* .
**Step 4:** Project *i*, which had been removed from the case
base, is added back.

Our first interesting observation is that, for DS1, results
obtained without adaptation were never statistically
significantly better than those obtained using adaptation
rules. In addition, whenever the use of adaptation rules
gave significantly better predictions, it occurred for the
three types of adaptation rules. This result is partially
similar to that obtained in [11]. Their dataset, which
presents very little collinearity and no outliers (and
therefore less “messy” than ours) showed best predictions
using no adaptation and adaptation without weights. The


commonality between results presented in [11] and ours,
for DS1, is that adaptation rules without weights showed
the best predictions for the less “messy” dataset.
Regarding DS2, no adaptation rules of any type gave
better prediction accuracy, which confirms the results
obtained in [11]. Our results show that most predictions
obtained without the use of adaptation rules presented
statistically significantly better results than those using
adaptation rules. The same trend occurs in [11] however
only for the Unweighted Euclidean distance. We believe

The results for FSS are presented in Tables 6 and 7.
Since DS1 had only one feature selected by
ArchANGEL it was meaningless to use adaptations with
weights or the Weighted Euclidean distance.
In general, using FSS improved prediction accuracy,
for both datasets, where in some cases the differences
were statistically significant. It is interesting to observe,
however, that those trends regarding adaptation rules
which were described in Section 5, seem also to be
present here. For DS1 all statistically significantly better
predictions were obtained using adaptation rules whereas
for DS2 the opposite trend occurs, i.e., all statistically
significantly better predictions were obtained without the
use of adaptation rules. This suggests that using FSS
generally improves prediction accuracy.
We also observed that when using FSS, DS2 did not
have as many “best predictions” as it did without FSS (19
out of 24 without FSS, 11 out of 24 using FSS). On the
other hand, for DS1, the number of “best predictions”
increased (5 out of 12 without FSS, to 8 out of 12 with
FSS). That may suggest that the use of FSS may help the
adaptation process, at least for the type of adaptation rules
we employed in this study.
In relation to the results presented in this paper, we
look at them optimistically as they presented similar
trends to those in [11]. In addition, obtaining similar


results based on quite different Web project datasets
(static vs. dynamic applications, different technologies,
different types of developers (real world Web developers,
postgraduate students), very different effort etc) also
seems to suggest that the trends we have observed in both
studies may indeed be able to be generalised. However, as
previously mentioned, further investigation is clearly
necessary in order to assess if these trends can be
generalised.

**7.** **Conclusions**

The contribution of this paper is mainly in replicating
a previous study using adaptation techniques and to
compare these results to those obtained using Feature
subset selection (FSS), looking at what extent FSS
improves accuracy given our datasets and the types of
projects employed.
We believe that the lessons to be learnt from our

results are as follows:

�� The types of adaptation rules employed in this study
seem to improve prediction accuracy if used on
datasets which are less “messy”. Our results were
similar to those in [11].
