**Abstract**

*Software practitioners recognise the importance of*
*realistic estimates of effort to the successful management*
*of software projects, the Web being no exception. Having*
*realistic estimates at an early stage in a project's life*
*cycle* *allow* *project* *managers* *and* *development*
*organisations to manage resources effectively.*
*Prediction is a necessary part of an effective process,*
*whether it be authoring, design, testing, or Web*
*development as a whole.*
*The first part of this paper describes a case study*
*evaluation (CSE) where we measured characteristics of*
*Web applications and the effort involved in designing and*
*authoring those applications. The second half explores*
*how we can use the measurements obtained to predict the*
*effort for design and author future Web applications. The*
*prediction models employed were two algorithmic models*

*- linear regression and stepwise multiple regression.*
*Although the models generated did not present good*
*prediction power, we believe that by using techniques that*
*are more flexible we will have better results.*

**1. Introduction**

In any scientific field, measurement generates
quantitative descriptions of processes and products,
making it possible to understand behaviour and result.
This understanding allows us to select better techniques
and tools to control and improve processes, products and
resources [1].
As engineering involves the analysis of measurements

[1], hypermedia engineering and, in particular, Web
engineering, will only become a true engineering


discipline when a solid foundation of measurement-based
theories is build.
We regard measurement as important for four basic
activities:
(i) understanding the development and
maintenance processes;
(ii) controlling software projects;
(iii) improving processes and products;
(iv) predicting processes;

Software practitioners recognise the importance of
realistic estimates of effort to the successful management
of software projects, the Web being no exception. Having
realistic estimates at an early stage in a project's life cycle
allow project managers and development organisations to
manage resources effectively.
Prediction is a necessary part of an effective process,
whether it be authoring, design, testing, or Web
development as a whole.
A prediction process involves:
� The capturing of data about past projects or even past
development phases within the same project.
� The identification of size metrics, cost drivers and the
formulation of theories about their relationship with
effort.
� The generation of prediction models to be applied to
projects.
� The assessment of how effective those prediction
models are.

This paper focuses on effort prediction for design and
authoring processes. We adopt the classification proposed
by Lowe and Hall [2] where authoring encompasses the
management of activities for the actual content and
structure of the application and its presentation. Design
covers the methods used for generating the structure and

**2. Background**

The original motivation for deriving and using
measures was provided by managers who wanted predict
project costs during early phases in the software lifecycle. As a result several models for cost and effort
estimation have been proposed and used [3].
Existing approaches to effort prediction fall into three
general categories [7], as follows:

- Expert judgement

- Algorithmic models

- Analogy

Expert judgement is a method widely used over the
years. However, the means of deriving an estimate are not
explicit and therefore not repeatable. Expert opinion,
although always difficult to quantify, can be an effective
estimating tool on its own or as an adjusting factor for
algorithmic models [8].
Algorithmic models, which have been to date the most
popular in the literature, attempt to represent the
relationship between effort and one or more project
characteristics. The main “cost driver” used in such a
model is usually taken to be some notion of the software
size (e.g. the number of lines of source code, number of
pages, number of links). Algorithmic models always need
to be calibrated or adjusted to local circumstances.
Examples of algorithmic models are the COCOMO model

[9], the SLIM model [10] and function points [11].
Estimation by analogy involves characterising the
project for which an estimate is required and use this


characterisation as a basis for finding similar or analogous
completed projects, for which effort is known. These
effort values are then used, possibly with adjustment, to
generate a predicted value of effort. Finding the right
analogies and assessing the degree of similarity are the
difficulties of this method.
An advantage of algorithmic models over estimation
and expert judgement is that they allow users to see how a
model derives its conclusions. This can be important for
the purpose of verification as well as theory building and
gaining an understanding of the process being modelled

[12]. However these models need to be calibrated to the
local environment in which they are used, which some
consider a disadvantage.
Recently several comparisons have been made
between the three categories of prediction models
presented in this Section, based on their prediction power

[12,13,14,15,16]. However no converging results were
obtained so far.

**3.1 The Case Study Evaluation**

The case study evaluation measured several attributes
(metrics) of Web applications and the effort involved in
Web design and authoring. The attributes reflect our
notion of product size, complexity, characteristics of the
application, process and resources that are expected to
influence effort in some way. A Product may represent a
Web application, Web page, program (cgi script etc.) or
media (video, audio, image etc).
The aim of the case study was to measure attributes for
use as parameters in a top-down effort estimation models

[3]. Top-down estimation begins with the overall process
or product. A full estimate is made, and estimates for the
component parts are calculated as relative portions of the
whole.
This case study was in part a replication of a case study
conducted two years ago at Southampton University, in
the UK [17].
This case study consisted of the design and authoring
of Web applications aimed at teaching a chosen topic.
These applications were structured according to the
Cognitive Flexibility Theory (CFT) principles [18] using
a minimum of 50 Web pages.
The main metaphor employed by the CFT is that of a
criss-crossed landscape. This metaphor suggests a nonlinear and multidimensional traversal of complex subject
matter, returning to the same place in the conceptual
landscape on different occasions, coming from different
directions. This is best supported, according to the CFT,
by the use of multiple representations and different

**3.3 Validity of the Case Study**

There are three types of threats to the validity of an
empirical study [21]:

- Construct validity: Represents to what extent the
predictor and response variables precisely measure the
concepts they claim to measure.

- Internal validity: Represents to what extent
conclusions can be drawn about the casual effect of the
predictor variables on the response variable.

- External validity: Represents the domain to which a
study’s findings can be generalised.

**3.3.1 Construct Validity.** 1) Design and authoring effort
are difficult concepts to measure. However, it is argued
that the data collected and the response variable derived
from it are intuitively reasonable measures. 2) There is no
standard on what constitutes the design and authoring
processes of a Web application and, therefore, the
activities used in this study may not be representative of
some practices in the Web community. 3) The metrics
collected, apart from effort, experience and structure,
were all objective and precisely quantifiable. The scale
used to measure experience and the three types of
structure of an application were described in detail on
both questionnaires.

**3.3.2 Internal Validity.** There were four confounding
factors in the case study evaluation:

i) Subjects' authoring and designing experience.
ii) Maturation effects, i.e. learning effects caused by
subjects learning as an experiment proceeds.
iii) Structure of the application.


iv) Tools used to help author and design the Web
application.

The data collected revealed that:

i) Subjects' authoring and design experiences were
mainly little (experience=2) or average (experience =
3). Based on the scales' descriptions given on both
questionnaires, going from level 2 to level 3 does not
require learning of quite different skills. A scatterplot
between experience and effort helped investigate their
relationship. Most datapoints fell within the interval
between 10 to 80 hours, either for experience 2 or 3,
and data points were clustered on similar areas.
Consequently we did not split the original dataset.
ii) Subjects had to develop a Web application as
part of their first assignment. They also received
training in the CFT principles. Consequently, it seems
that the maturation effects were controlled, or at least
substantially reduced.
iii) Hierarchy was the structure used by nearly all
the subjects.
iv) Notepad (or similar text editor) and FirstPage
were the two tools used the most (see Figure 5:
Notepad is identified as number 2 and FirstPage as
number 3). Notepad is a very simple text editor.
FirstPage is a freeware software which offers buttons
related to HTML tags, which means that the user does
not need to remember the tags. Although they differ
with respect to the functionality offered, a scatterplot
between total-effort and tool revealed that at most
datapoints fell within the interval between 10 to 80
hours, either using Notepad or FirstPage, and the data
points were clustered on similar areas. Consequently,
it seems that the confounding effects that the tools
would have caused were reduced.

2. The selection effects, which are due to natural
variations in subject performance, were controlled.
3. The instrumentation effects in general did not occur in
this evaluation. The questionnaires used were the same
and the same hypermedia system was used to develop the
applications.

**3.3.3 External Validity.** 1) The results may be domain
dependent as all the subjects answered the questionnaires
based on their experience in developing Web applications
for education. This evaluation should therefore be
repeated in domains other than education if the results are
to be generalised to other domains.
2) The experimental scale [22] is a threat when the
experimental setting or the materials are not
representative of industrial practice. The applications
developed presented good quality interface and contents,
when compared to other Web applications found in
industry, as observed by one of the authors when marking
the applications. Therefore, from this perspective it seems
that the applications developed are representative of
industrial practice.
3) The applications did not have more than 103
documents (mean=57) and 1501 links (mean 616). These
numbers represent medium size applications, according to

[2]. Consequently they are not representative of the large
Web applications for education developed by some
organisations, in general. On the other hand, the
applications developed had a similar to or even better
interface and contents quality than Web applications
developed by professionals (as observed by one of the
authors when marking the applications) which seems to
indicate that the results of the case study are likely to
scale-up.
4) A threat to subject generalizability [22] may exist
when the subject population is not drawn from the wider
population. The subjects that participated in the case
study were either fourth year students doing an Honours
Degree in Computing or MSc students. It is likely that
they present skills similar to Web professionals who are
in the beginning of their careers. The use of students as
subjects, while sometimes considered unrealistic, is
justified for two reasons: firstly, empirical evidence by
Bohem-Davis and Ross [23] indicates that students are
equal to professionals in many quantifiable measures,
including their approach to developing software;
secondly, for pragmatic considerations, having students as
subjects was the only viable option for this case study.

**4. Estimation Process**

Once the data was collected two algorithmic models,
linear regression and stepwise regression, were used to
generate bottom-up and top-down effort prediction
models for Web design and authoring.
Two prediction models were generated, one for each
statistical technique employed.
After obtaining the prediction models, it was important
to assess each model's accuracy (how reliable their


predictions were) by measuring their prediction power.
We measured their prediction power using the Mean
Magnitude of Relative Error (MMRE) [15], described in
detail in Section 4.3.

**4.1 Linear Regression and Stepwise Regression**

The general purpose of linear and stepwise multiple
regression models is to learn more about the relationship
between several predictor variables and a response
variable. Multiple regression and linear regression allow
the researcher to ask (and hopefully answer) the general
question "what is the best predictor of ..." [24].
Regression modelling is one of the most widely used
statistical modelling techniques for fitting a quantitative
response variable *y* as a function of one or more predictor
variables *x* *1* *, x* *2* *,...,x* *p* . Regression models are widely used
because they often provide an excellent fit to a response
variable when the true functional relationship, if any,
between the response and the predictors is unknown [24].

In the scope of this paper the response variable is *Total*
*Effort* .

One of the main aspects of Table 9 is that stepwise
regression, contrary to that expected, is not consistently
better than linear regression. A similar result has also
been reported in Shepperd et al. [7], where they suggest
the following explanation for the phenomenon:
Regression analysis is based upon minimising the sum of
the squares of the residuals. A residual is the difference
between actual effort and predicted effort. As the MMRE
is based upon the average of the sum of the residuals,
poor predictions have far more influence when trying to
fit a regression equation than they do when assessing the
overall predictive performance of the method. This can
lead to small anomalies in the relative performance of
linear regression and stepwise regression models.
It is generally accepted that algorithmic models
perform better on smaller more homogenous datasets.
Although the number of Web pages for each application
was similar, other variables had a great variation and this
may have influenced the results obtained. For example,
*Total Page Allocation* varied from 129 to 728 and *Media*
*Count* varied from 0 to 296. Splitting the original datasets
in an effort to make them more homogeneous could make
the corresponding sub-datasets so reduced that the side


effect would be not being able to find statistically
significant regression models.

**5. Conclusions and Future Work**

This paper has presented a case study evaluation in
which we measured attributes to be used on top-down and
bottom-up effort predictions. The datasets obtained
represent attributes of several entities, namely
Application, Page, Media and Program. We collected
information on 37 applications, 2165 pages, 443 media
reused, 1027 media not reused, 115 programs reused and
22 programs not reused. Some of the suggested metrics
can be used in the prediction of effort for dynamically
generated Web applications ( *Program Count*, *Total Code*
*Length*, *Reused Program Count*, *Total Reused Code*
*Length*, *Structuring Effort*, *Interface Planning* and
*Interface Building* ). However, the metrics were mostly
related to static Web applications.
In addition, the paper proposes effort prediction
models which were generated using two statistical
techniques, namely Linear regression and Stepwise
Multiple Regression.

Although the prediction models generated did not in
general present low MMREs, we are aware that other
techniques, such as General Linear Models (GLMs),
which have the advantage of allowing relationships
between response and predictor variables to be non-linear,
provide considerable flexibility while still yielding to a
rigorous statistical treatment. In addition, there are also
other non-algorithmic techniques which, in the same way
as linear models, allow the user to see how a model
derives its conclusions [12]. These techniques are fuzzy
systems and case-based reasoning. The use of GLMs and
case-based reasoning represent the scope of our future
work.
We also plan to apply effort prediction to the whole
development cycle of Web applications and to compare
the performance of several prediction techniques against
human estimation.
To conclude, there is an urgent need for adequate Web
development effort prediction at an early stage in the
development. As the use of the Web as a resource
delivery environment increases, effort estimation can
contribute significantly to the reduction of costs and time
involved in developing Web applications.
