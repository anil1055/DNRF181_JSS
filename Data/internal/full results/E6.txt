Predicting the effort required in developing Web applications, although a difficult task, plays

an important role in the successful management of Web development projects.

This paper has two objectives: i) the first one is to describe a quantitative case study which

measured a set of proposed effort metrics and also the effort involved in authoring Web

(World Wide Web) applications; and ii) the second one is to suggest a Generalised Linear

Model (GLM) for effort prediction of Web applications' authoring.

Results have shown that, based on the dataset used, three (half) of the proposed metrics

should be included in the effort model generated.

**1.** **Introduction**

Since the beginning of this decade the World Wide Web (Web) has become the best known

example of a hypermedia system. Numerous organisations world-wide have developed so far

thousands of commercial and/or educational Web sites (applications) [1] . However, developing

good quality Web sites has a high cost, mostly in time and amount of difficulty involved for

the authors (Brown 1995). Consequently, by applying measurement principles to measure the

quality of Web sites and their development process, feedback can be obtained to help

improve products and processes.

In addition, software practitioners in general recognise the importance of accurate estimates

of development effort to the successful management of major software projects. The Web is

no exception. By having realistic estimates at early stages in a project life cycle project

managers and Web development organisations can manage resources properly.

1 Web site is a synonym for Web application, which represent the end result of Web development process.

1

To address these issues, this paper presents the results of a quantitative case study which

empirically validates a set of metrics proposed to measure the effort involved in authoring

Web applications. Authoring is considered a subset of the whole Web development,

characterised by managing the actual content and structure of the application and how it is

presented. The paper also investigates, based on the data collected in the case study, the use

of Generalised Linear Models to estimate the effort involved in authoring Web applications.

The proposed metrics adhere to the representational theory of measurement and have been

theoretically validated according to the validation framework proposed by Kitchenham et al.

(1995b). The theoretical validation is presented in (Mendes 1999).

The case study is described in Section 2 and Section 3 presents the Generalised Linear Model

for effort. Finally, we give our conclusions and comments on future work in Section 4.

**2.** **The Quantitative Case Study**

**2.1** **Description**

Two questionnaires were used in the case study. The first one - Q1 - asked subjects to rate

their level of Web authoring experience on a scale from 0 (no experience) to 10 (a great deal

of experience). The second one - Q2 - (see Appendix A) was used to measure the predictor

and response variables. Members of the research group checked both questionnaires for

ambiguous questions, unusual tasks, definitions in the appendix and number of questions.

The subjects who participated in the case study were 76 second-year Computer Science

students from the University of Southampton attending the Human-Computer Interaction

course. An analysis of the questionnaires obtained showed that six were missing fundamental

information. Consequently, these samples were not used in the data analysis. The subjects

were given coursework that consisted of developing Web applications to teach about Human
Computer Interaction concepts. They were also required to structure their applications

according to the Cognitive Flexibility Theory (CFT) (Spiro et al. 1995) principles, and to

create the applications using a minimum of 50 documents.

In order to reduce any learning effects (Kitchenham et al. 1995a) subjects were given an

initial task – to author their personal Web pages. Two Web tutorials were suggested so that it

would be possible for subjects with no Web authoring experience to learn how to author Web

minutes).

Questionnaire Q2 was handed in together with the coursework. This questionnaire had two

sections: one to collect attributes of the application and another to collect the *elapsed time*

spent in authoring the application. Authoring was sub-divided into several sub-tasks, as

**2.2** **Response and Predictor variables**

The predictor variables (metrics) used in the case study evaluation were: *hyperdocument size*,

*reused-documents, connectivity*, *perceived* *compactness*, *perceived stratum* and *structure of*

*the application* ; The response variable used to measure authoring effort was the estimated

*elapsed time* (number of hours) it took each subject to author the Web application.

*Hyperdocument size* refers to the number of documents that each Web application has.

Documents are HTML files, and a Web application is a collection of documents designed

with a specific aim. In our case, the domain considered to be representative was education.

The Web applications considered are ‘static’, where the number of dynamically generated

pages is quite small, if not absent.

*Reused-documents* represent the number of HTML files which have not been created from

scratch.

3

*Connectivity* refers to the number of links that the Web application has. The links considered

here are either structural or referential links (Conklin 1987), leaving apart dynamically

computed links.

*Compactness* (Botafogo et al. 1992) indicates how inter-connected the documents are. A

completely connected application means that, from each document, there are links reaching

all the other documents in the application. Compactness was measured in the case study

evaluation by asking subjects to give an estimate of it, from 1 (completely disconnected) to 5

(completely connected). Consequently, the compactness measured was the perceived

compactness ( *P-Compactness* ).

*Stratum* (Botafogo et al. 1992) indicates to what degree the Web application is organised for

directed reading. A sequential application represents an application where there is only one

path to be followed by the reader. For the case study evaluation, subjects were asked to

estimate the stratum of the application, from 1 (no sequential navigation) to 5 (sequential

navigation). Consequently, the stratum measured was the perceived stratum ( *P-Stratum* ).

*Structure of the application* represents the way in which the documents have been linked:

sequence, hierarchy, and network (Whalley 1990). A sequential structure corresponds to

documents linearly linked; a hierarchical structure denotes documents linked in a tree shape

and a network structure for documents linked in a net shape.

**2.3** **Validity of the Case Study**

This subsection presents the validity of the case study based on the three types of threats to

the validity of an empirical study (Kitchenham et al. 1995a):

1. Construct validity: Represents to what extent the predictor and response variables

precisely measure the concepts they claim to measure.

2. Internal validity: Represents to what extent conclusions can be drawn about the causal

effect of the predictor variables on the response variables.

3. External validity: Represents the domain to which a study’s findings can be generalised.

**Construct Validity**

The following problems might threaten the construct validity of the experiment:

1. *P-Compactness* and *P-stratum* were estimated by subjects, rather than directly measured

in the applications. It can be argued that estimated values are not ideal and may weaken

4

the results obtained. However, it would not be feasible to ask subjects to calculate

themselves those measures as it would be very time-consuming. *Compactness*, as

proposed by Botafogo et al. (1992), also uses an estimated value on its calculation (the

conversion constant), leading to subjective measures as well. Finally, *stratum*, as

proposed by Botafogo et al. (1992) is described by the authors as “not a perfect metric,

where some problems are evident”. Therefore, we decided to re-use these concepts and

measure them in a different way.

2. Although the number of documents has been identified by hypermedia authors as a

valuable metric to measure *hyperdocument size* (Mendes 1999), we are aware that there

are other metrics which can also be used (for example, the length of the application, the

complexity of documents and the functionality offered) and they will be the focus of our

next evaluation.

3. Although the questionnaire does not describe all the scale point definitions, guidance was

given to students during the classes.

**Internal Validity**

The following problems might threaten the internal validity of the experiment:

1. There were three confounding factors in this evaluation: i) subjects’ experience; ii)

maturation effects, which are learning effects caused by subjects learning as an

experiment proceeds; and iii) tools used to help author/manage the Web application. The

data collected showed that: i) the experiences did vary greatly. Consequently, it was

decided to consider two separate groups – subjects with experience levels ranging from 1

to 5 (41 subjects) – LEL group - and subjects with experience levels ranging from 6 to 10

(29 subjects) – HEL group; ii) subjects were asked to create their Web pages before

authoring the applications and they all received training in the CFT principles.

Consequently, it seems that the maturation effects were controlled, or at least reduced;

and iii) the number of tools used did not vary greatly. This result was confirmed

statistically.

2. The selection effects, which are due to natural variations in subject performance, were

controlled.

3. The instrumentation effects in general did not occur in this evaluation. The questionnaires

used were the same and the same system was used to author the applications.

5

**External Validity**

The following problems might limit the external validity of the experiment:

1. The results may be domain dependent as all the subjects answered the questionnaires

based on their experience in authoring Web applications for education. This evaluation

should therefore be repeated in domains other than education if the results are to be

generalised to other domains.

2. The experimental scale (Porter et al. 1997) is a threat when the experimental setting or the

materials are not representative of industrial practice. The applications authored presented

good quality interface and contents, when compared to other Web applications found in

industry. Therefore, from this perspective it seems that those applications are

representative of industrial practice, at least as far as Web authoring is concerned.

3. The applications did not have more than 100 documents and 900 links, on average, as the

subjects were supposed to spend only three working days on the coursework. These

numbers represent small applications and are probably not representative of the large

Web applications for education authored by some organisations, in general. On the other

hand, the applications developed had a similar to or even better interface and contents

quality than Web applications created by professionals, as noticed by one of the authors

when marking the Web applications authored by the students. These results seem to

indicate that the results of the case study are likely to scale-up.

4. A threat to subject generalizability (Porter et al. 1997) may exist when the subject

population is not drawn from the wider population. The subjects that participated in the

case study may not be representative of Web professionals, since second-year Computer

Science students cannot be categorised as experienced Web authors. The use of students

as subjects, while sometimes considered unrealistic, is justified for two reasons: firstly,

empirical evidence by Bohem-Davis and Ross (1992) indicates that students are equal to

professionals in many quantifiable measures, including their approach to developing

software; secondly, for pragmatic considerations, having students as subjects was the only

viable option for this case study.

**2.4** **Generalised Linear Model for Effort**

We chose to model effort using the class of generalised linear models (GLMs) described in

McCullagh and Nelder (1989). These models have the advantage of allowing relationships

between response and predictor variables to be non-linear, providing considerable flexibility

while still yielding to a rigorous statistical treatment.

6

In its most general form, a GLM consists of a response variable, *Y*, together with *p* predictor

variables, *x* 1 *,...,x* *p* Here, *Y* might be a measurement of authoring effort for an application,
# while x 1, ..., x p  represent properties of the application that affect the effort required, such as

application size or complexity. A probability distribution is proposed for *Y*, chosen from a

group of so-called exponential distributions (McCullagh and Nelder 1989). This group

includes the familiar Gaussian, Gamma, Poisson and Binomial distributions, ensuring that a

wide range of both skewed and symmetric distributional shapes can be accommodated.

The dependence of *Y* on the predictor variables *x* 1 *,...,x* *p* is captured by modelling a

transformation of the expected value of *Y* as a linear function of the predictors. We represent

this as

*g* ( *E* ( *Yi* )) =  0 +  1 *x* 1 *i* + ... +  *p* *x* *pi*, (1)

where *Y* i is the *i* th instance of the response variable *Y*, and *E(Y* *i* *)* denotes the mean of the

distribution of *Y* *i* . The quantities *x* 1 *i*,..., *x* *pi* denote the *i* th instances of variables *x* 1,..., *x* *p*

respectively, and  *0* *,...,*  *p* are parameters to be estimated. The transformation *g* is a monotone

function, and must be invertible over the whole real line. Since *E(Y* *i* *)* will generally be

nonnegative in the present context, a suitable choice for g is the logarithmic function, which

maps the nonnegative numbers onto the reals. The function g controls the nature of the non
linear relationship between the response and predictor variables.

Any GLM is specified by the two components above: a selected form for the probability

distribution of *Y* (e.g. Gaussian, Poisson, etc.), and a transformation g that models the mean

of this distribution as a function of the predictor variables *x* 1 *,...,x* *p* . The first component

specifies the nature of the randomness inherent in observing *Y* - for example, whether *Y*

comes from a symmetric Gaussian distribution and is equally likely to fall above or below the

distribution mean, or whether the distribution of *Y* is skewed and departures from the

distribution mean can be greater on one side than the other. The second component fixes the

location of the distribution of *Y*, through knowledge of the other variables. On average, larger

web applications are likely to require more effort than smaller ones; thus we use attributes

such as application size to model the mean value of *Y* .

7

The GLM framework incorporates ordinary least squares regression as a special case, in

which the error distribution of *Y* is Gaussian and the transformation g is the identity.

Common criticisms of ordinary least squares regression are the restrictive need for a linear

relationship between predictors and response, and the assumption of constant variance in the

errors (Gray and MacDonell 1997). These problems are overcome by casting the models as

more sophisticated GLMs.

The methods described in this section were carried out using the statistical software package

S-PLUS (Statistical Sciences, Inc. 1991).

**Effort** **model**

We formulated the effort GLM as follows. The variable *Y* was the total time, in hours, taken

to author the application. The predictor variables, detailed in section 2.2, were:

  - *x* 1 : experience. A categorical variable denoting whether the application was authored

by a subject with low experience (LEL) or high experience (HEL).

  - *x* 2 : application size (number of new documents in the application).

  - *x* 3 : connectivity (number of links in the application).

  - *x* 4 : reused documents (number of reused documents).

  - *x* 5 : compactness (quantitative variable measured on a scale from 1 to 5).

  - *x* 6 : stratum (quantitative variable measured on a scale from 1 to 5).

  - *x* 7 : structure (categorical variable classifying the application as a sequence, hierarchy

or network).

The probability distribution of the response *Y* was chosen to be gamma. This distribution is

right skewed, allowing for the possibility of severe overrun on a time schedule. The variance

of the distribution is proportional to the square of the mean - thus as the expected duration of

a project increases, so does the associated uncertainty. This is certainly true in practice, since

it is far more difficult to estimate a precise duration for a two-year project than for a two-day

one (Grey et al. 1999). Use of the gamma distribution for software development estimation

was also advocated by Kitchenham and Linkman (1996).

We used a logarithmic transformation g as explained above, so that *g(E(Y* *i* *)) = log(E(Y* *i* *))* .

For model selection, we used Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC), which seeks the best fit

8

to the data while penalising overparameterisation; see Venables and Ripley (1999) for details.

The AIC procedure suggested that only variables *x* 1, *x* 2 and *x* 3 should be included in the

model, providing the final formulation

*log(E(Y* *i* *)) =*  *0* *+*  1 *x* *1i* *+*  *2* *x* *2i* *+*  *3* *x* *3i* (2)

Here, *x* 1 *i* is 0 for group LEL and 1 for group HEL, while the other variables are the

measurements described earlier.

The effect of using a logarithmic transformation g is clear from equation (2). The experience

factor makes a multiplicative contribution to the mean *E(Y* *i* *)*, so that a subject with low

experience might be expected to take (say) twice as long as one with high experience. This is

in contrast to an additive effect, in which a subject with low experience would always take

(say) 10 hours longer than one with high experience, whatever the expected duration of the

project. The multiplicative formulation is more realistic in this context. The contributions to

the mean of application size and connectivity are non-linear. Examination of the data

suggests that this is a reasonable postulate for the true relationship.

The AIC model selection procedure selected a relatively small subset of predictor variables

for inclusion in the final model: namely experience, number of new documents in the

application, and number of links in the application. These variables are all clearly defined

and easy to evaluate. Some of the omitted variables, however, such as compactness, stratum,

and structure, are more difficult to define precisely. Although the student subjects were given

guidance in classes as to the correct classification of their applications, it is likely that there

were still some inconsistencies in the reporting of these variables, and this could account for

the failure of the model to select them as important predictors. More explicit guidance for

evaluating these variables, and more comprehensive verification of the reported

measurements, is being carried out in a new study currently underway.

In relation to the prediction power of our model, our comments are as follows:

- Effort was modelled using a probability distribution that has quite a lot of variation.

Consequently, despite the fact that the errors are apparently quite large, they are still

within the sampling variability expected under the model. The model reflects the extent of

the uncertainty associated with the effort being modelled.

- Literature in the field of effort prediction often uses the Mean Magnitude of Relative

Error (MMRE) to measure the prediction power of the models used (Myrtveit and

Stensrud 1999; Shepperd and Schofield 1997). However, within the scope of our work,

10

- we do not consider the MMRE a very good measure of how good the model is, because it

does not make any allowance for the uncertainty associated with the effort.

- Instead, the 'deviance' is used as a measure of fit. The deviance evaluates how likely the

observations are under the fitted model, rather than what the absolute or relative error is

between prediction and observation. Consequently, the deviance does allow for the

natural variability: an observation can still be quite likely under the model, despite being

a long way from the prediction, because the model specifies that the variability is such

that even observations quite a long way from their predictions are to be expected.

- We used the deviance to fit and select the model. This means that our model has been

selected as optimal among the various alternatives we provided for the model-fitting

software.

- In conclusion, we believe that exact predictions of effort are very difficult, if not

impossible, to obtain, and that it is more useful to get a realistic impression of the bounds

of variability associated with any prediction.

**3.** **Conclusions**

This paper has reported i) a quantitative case study aimed at measuring proposed metrics and

ii) the proposal of an effort estimation model using Generalised Linear Model. The case study

involved the authoring of Web applications using the principles of the Cognitive Flexibility

Theory. The data were collected using questionnaires, which were answered by the subjects

who participated in the case study.

Although the parameter estimates generated for our GLM model can only be applied to the

dataset used, The GLM model can be applied to any Web projects, as long as it is fitted

individually for each new organisation or set of projects.

We believe that the use of generalised linear models to model effort provides a flexible

modelling framework, for the following reasons:

1) Choice of response distribution. We used gamma, although there are a variety of other

options.

2) Choice of the function g, which is determined to some extent by the choice made in (1).

3) Choice of the predictor variables, which can also be transformed.

11

In terms of further work, the two datasets used in the case study had four variables

characterising in some sense the applications. However, as size can be described in terms of

length, functionality and complexity (Fenton and Pfleeger 1997), further investigation is

necessary to identify the best combination of variables that give best results.

In addition, the datasets used in this study were collected 2 years ago and unfortunately, we

were unable to get hold of some applications in order to validate the reliability of our data.

For this reason, we are currently implementing a tool that measures our proposed metrics

automatically. Our aim is to use this tool in our next evaluation, which will be a replication of

the case study presented in this paper.

Finally, we are also investigating the use of the GLM as a framework for risk management.

GLM, in common with all regression models, estimates a full probability distribution for the

response variable *Y* . Using this knowledge for all proposed projects, we believe that we can

determine the required level of risk for each one in order to attain an acceptable collective

risk level across all projects.
