**1. INTRODUCTION**

Effort estimation is a critical activity for planning and monitoring
software project development and for delivering the product on
time and within budget. Significant over or under-estimates can
be very expensive for a company. Consequently, it is paramount
for the competitiveness of a software company to be able to
effectively predict in advance the effort required to develop a
software project. Nowadays, the growth in complexity and
number of provided features makes the effort estimation a crucial
activity also for web application development ([10], [15], [16],

[17], [19], [21], [24], [25]).

In the literature a lot of different methods to estimate software

development cost have been proposed, conventionally divided in
*Model* and *Non-Model* *Based Methods* . While the latter mainly
take into account expert judgments (thus with highly-subjective
factors), Model-Based methods rely on a formal approach,
involving the application of some algorithms to a number of
inputs, to produce an effort estimate. The inputs for these
algorithms are factors that influence the development effort of
software projects. Among these, *Software Size* is a widely
accepted key cost driver. For traditional desktop applications,
several size measures have been conceived to be employed in
effort/cost models. Among these, *Function Points* ( *FPs* ) have
achieved a wide acceptance to estimate the size of business
systems and to indirectly predict the project effort, cost, and
duration [3]. But, when dealing with dynamic web applications,
there is not yet a widely accepted size measure, even if a lot of
research is currently devoted to this issue ([1], [2], [4], [15], [16],

[17], [18], [19], [21], [24], [25]).

In addition, several techniques have been proposed in the
literature to build effort prediction models, such as Case-Based
Reasoning or Analogy-Based Estimation, Linear Regression, and
Regression Tree ([5], [6], [7], [11], [12], [23], [26]). Also in the
context of web engineering, some empirical studies have been
undertaken so far to establish the most suitable techniques for
model construction ([5], [6]).

It is widely recognized that several investigations should be
performed to verify/confirm empirical results, especially using
data coming from industrial world. To this aim, in the paper we
will report on an empirical analysis we have carried out relying on
data coming from 15 web projects developed by an Italian
software company. The analysis aims to gain insight on both the
size measures that could be considered effective indicators in

predicting development efforts of web applications, and on the
effectiveness of the modeling techniques. In particular, we have
employed some *size* measures that have been proposed in the


9

literature for early estimations, which can be grouped into two
categories: *Length Measures* (such as number of pages, medias,
server side scripts, etc…) and *Functional Measures* (such as
external input, external output, external query, web building
blocks, etc…) ([1], [2], [15], [17], [19],[24]).

About the techniques, we have exploited the *Linear Regression*,
*Regression Tree*, *Analogy-Based Estimation*, a combination of
Regression Tree and Linear Regression, and a combination of
Regression Tree and Analogy-Based Estimation, in order to
determine which one provides the best prediction.

It is worth to point out that in the paper we focus on the Size
Measures as cost factors. Thus, other factors, such as company
expertise, client understanding of the needs, etc, are left to further
investigations. Moreover, our goal is to provide an insight into
both techniques and size measures exploiting industrial data,
rather than to define a wide-range, general purpose model for web
applications.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2
the data sets used for the empirical evaluation and the considered
Size Measures are described. Section 3 presents the empirical
evaluation we carried out by exploiting various modeling
techniques. Then, the comparison of the empirical results is
reported in Section 4. Some final remarks and comparisons with
related work conclude the paper.

**2. THE DATASET**

To perform the empirical analysis we have exploited data on 15
web applications, developed by a medium-sized Italian software
company. The core business of this company is the development
of enterprise information systems, mainly for local and central
government. Among other clients, there are educational structures,
health organizations, research centers, industries, and other public
institutions. It is highly specialized in the design, development,
and management of solutions for web portals, enterprise
intranet/extranet applications (such as content-ware, e-commerce,
work-flow managers, etc…), and Geographical/Environmental
Information Systems. The company, established in 1984, has
about fifty employees, and is certified ISO 9001 for software
development. Its turnover in the year 2003 was about 5M €.

The web projects used for the empirical study can be classified in
the following domains: e-government, e-banking, web portals, and
intranet applications. They were developed in the last four years,
exploiting a wide range of web-oriented technologies such as
J2EE, ASP, ASP.NET, etc… Oracle was the commonly adopted
DBMS, but also SQL Server, Access and MySQL were employed
in some projects. All the projects match the definition of “web
application” provided by Conallen in [8].

The information related to the projects about the considered
measures and the actual effort required for the development
process have been obtained both from analysis and design
documents, by using questionnaires filled in by the project
managers of the software company. In the following, we will
describe the measures considered for the empirical analysis.

**3.1 OLS regression**
Linear Regression analysis is one of the most commonly used
statistical techniques for exploring the relationship between a
dependent variable and one or more independent variables [22].
In particular, we considered the multiple Linear Regression (LR)
analysis and a forward Stepwise Regression (SR) which allowed
us to compute linear regression in stages [22]. To evaluate the
goodness of fit of a regression model, several indicators have been
considered. Among them, the square of the linear correlation
coefficient, *R* [2], indicates the amount of the variance of the
dependent variable explained by the model related to the
independent variable. Other useful indicators are the *F* value and
the corresponding *p-value* (denoted by *Sign F* ), whose high and
low values, respectively, denote a high degree of confidence for
the prediction. We have also considered the *p-values* and *t-values*
for the corresponding coefficients and the intercept. The *p-values*
give an insight into the accuracy of the coefficients and the
intercept, whereas their *t-values* allow us to evaluate their
importance for the generated model. In particular, *p-values* less
than 0.05 are considered an acceptable threshold, meaning that the
variables are significant predictors with a confidence of 5%. As
for the *t-value*, a variable is significant if its corresponding value
is greater than 1.5.

For our case study, we applied the OLS regression to the dataset
of 15 web projects. Table 3 (a) shows the models obtained by
applying a multiple Linear Regression analysis (M1 and M2) and
a forward Stepwise Regression (M3 and M4) to Length and
Functional Measures. To identify the independent variables to be
employed in the LR models, we considered the predictors that are
statistically correlated to the effort ( *EFH* ) at level 0.05, carrying
out both Spearman’s rho and Pearson correlations. For all the
analyses we verified the constancy of the residual variance
( *homoscedasticity* ) by a Breush-Pagan test, which revealed that
the null hypothesis of homoscedasticity cannot be rejected, since
the *p-values* were greater than 0.05. Aanlogusly, the *normality*
assumption of the residuals was confirmed by a Shapiro-Wilk test.
In particular, a log transformation was necessary for Length


Measures. As for outliers, our analysis revealed that there were
not extreme values, which might improperly influence the models
(as reported by Cook’s distance test).

The LR method has identified for the model M1 the number of the

Server-Side scripts and Applications ( *SSApp* ) as the main factor
affecting the development effort. This makes sense, since we are
dealing with dynamic web applications. The second affecting
independent variable is the number of References to External
Modules ( *EL* ). This again makes sense, due to the nature of the
considered applications and their underlying technologies, heavily
interacting with external components. The obtained model is
characterized by a quite high *R* [2] value and a slightly low *F* value.
Both *t-value* and *p-value* for *SSApp* satisfy the acceptance threshold,
while this does not hold for the variable *EL* (Table 3 (b)).

**3.2 Regression Tree**
Regression Tree (RT) is a variant of decision trees that can be
used to approximate real-valued functions [5], [6], [17]. This
technique takes as input a set of numerical variables and generates
a regression tree predicting the value of the target variable
(corresponding to the dependent variable in linear regression). In
particular, a binary tree is built by recursively splitting the input
data into partitions. At the beginning all data are associated to the
root. Then, they are split in two parts, minimizing the sum of the
squared deviations from the mean in the separated parts. At each
split, the process determines the input variables to be used for
splitting, and the set of input values of the variable to associate to
the left and right child nodes, respectively. Let us observe that
each node has associated the mean value of the target variable.
The process ends when, for each node, a minimum size specified
for the node by the user is obtained. Subsequently, to determine
the predicted value for the target variable, we start from the root
node and then we follow the right or left branch, based on the
value of the splitting variable. We continue until a leaf node is
reached, which contains the predicted value.

As for our empirical study, it is worth noting that the application
of the technique on Length Measures has determined the splitting
of the group on the variable *SSApp* . Thus, also this method
suggested the number of Server-Side scripts and Applications to
be the main Length variable affecting the development effort for a
web application. Indeed, the left child node 2 (mean *EFH*
=1962.3) can be reached if *SSApp* ≤ 63 and had 7 rows associated,
while the right child node 3 (mean *EFH* = 3304) can be reached if
SSApp>63 and had 8 rows associated. On the other hand, when
considering Functional Measures, the technique split the group on
the variable *EI* . Again, also this method suggested the number of
User Inputs to be the chief Functional variable. In this case the
left child node 2 (mean *EFH* = 2024), had 7 rows associated and a
case goes left if *EI* ≤ 17. The right child node 3 (mean *EFH* =
3250), had 8 rows and a case goes right if *EI* - 17.

**3.3 Analogy-Based Estimation**
The idea behind the use of Analogy-based Estimation (AE) is to
predict the effort of a new project by considering similar projects
previously developed. In particular, completed projects are
characterized in terms of a set of *p* features and form the *case*
*base* . The new project is also characterized in terms of the same *p*
attributes and it is referred as the *target case* . Then, the similarity
between the target case and the other cases in the *p* -dimensional
feature space is measured, and the most similar cases (or projects)
are used, possibly with adaptations to obtain a prediction for the
target case [11], [26]. To apply the method, we had to select: the
relevant project features, the appropriate similarity function, the
number of analogies to select the similar projects to consider for
estimation, and the analogy adaptation strategy for generating the


estimation. It is worth to point out that the selection of the
similarity function and the number of analogies are crucial
decisions. Many researchers suggested to use Euclidean distance
as similarity measure and 1, 2, and 3 analogies to identify similar
projects ([5], [6], [17], [19]). The analogy adaptation step allows
for deciding how to obtain estimation after the most similar cases
have been determined. Widely used adaptation techniques are the
nearest neighbor ([5], [19]), the mean of the closest analogies

[17], [19], [23]), the inverse distance weighted mean, and inverse
rank weighted mean [26].

In our case study, to apply the Analogy-based Estimation
technique, we have exploited the ANGEL tool [26] on the dataset
of 15 web projects. To calculate the estimation, ANGEL allows
the Measurer to choose the relevant predictors, the similarity
function, the number of analogies, and the analogy adaptation
technique. We did not use its *subset selection* feature, since it
might be inefficient, as reported in [5], [26]. In particular, we
have considered all the selected variables of Table 1 as set of

features, since they are all relevant factors for designers and
developers. About the similarity function, ANGEL implements
the Euclidean distance using variables normalized between 0 and
1. Selecting the number of analogies is a key task, since it refers to
the number of similar cases to use for estimating the effort
required by the target case. Since we dealt with a small data set,
we used 1, 2, and 3 analogies, as suggested by many researchers
([5], [6], [17], [19]). In order to select similar projects for the
estimation, we employed the following adaptation strategies:
mean of k analogies (simple average), inverse distance weighted
mean, and inverse rank weighted mean [26].

To obtain the prediction, the tool adopts a *jack-knifing* procedure
on the dataset. Consequently, in our empirical study, ANGEL
generated 15 predictions, and the corresponding residuals, for
each selection of the number of analogies and of the analogy
adaptation technique. Each prediction has been obtained by
selecting a target project from the dataset, and by considering as
case base the other 14 projects. For the sake of space we cannot
report these results in the paper. However, the prediction
accuracies are compared in Section 4.

**4. COMPARISON OF THE PREDICTION**

**ACCURACY**

The above modeling techniques allowed us to build some
estimation models. In order to analyze the prediction accuracy of
these models we applied a *cross validation* (see, e.g. [5], [6])
technique (in particular a *leave-1-out* cross validation) by
partitioning the whole data set into two randomly selected sets:
the *training set* of 14 projects for model building and the *test set*
consisting of the remaining project for model evaluation.

To assess the acceptability of the derived effort prediction models,
we have used *MMRE*, *MdMRE*, *Pred* (l) [7], together with
*boxplots of residuals*, and *boxplots of z* [12] *.* In the following, we
compare the gathered empirical results taking into account first
the summary statistics and then the boxplots.

**4.1 Comparison using summary statistics**
The *Magnitude of Relative Error* [7] is defined as *MRE* =
| *EFHreal* — *EFHpred* | / *EFHreal*, where *EFHreal* and *EFHpred*
are the actual and the predicted efforts, respectively. This value
has been calculated for each test set derived from the *leave-1-out*

cross validation, using the delivered models. Then, the resulting
values have been aggregated across all the test sets, by taking into
account the Mean ( *MMRE* ) and the Median ( *MdMRE* ) [17].
*Prediction at level* *l*, is defined as *Pred* ( *l* ) = *k* / *N*, where *k* is the
number of observations with *MRE* less than or equal to *l*, and *N* is
the total number of observations. *MMRE* and *Pred* ( *l* ) are
considered the de facto standard to assess prediction models [7],
used in many comparisons (such as [4],[5],[6],[7],

[12],[17],[23],[25]). According to Conte *et al.*, a good effort
prediction model should have a *MMRE* ≤ 0.25 and *Pred* (0.25) ≥
0.75 (i.e., at least 75% of the predicted values should fall within
25% of their actual values) [9].

LR, SR, RT, and RT + LR are shown in Table 5, while the ones
for the Analogy-based Estimations are provided in Table 6 (at the

**4.2 Comparison using Boxplots**
Recently, Kitchenham et al. in [13] argued that boxplots of
residuals ( *EFHreal* — *EFHpred* ) and the boxplots of z (where z=
*EFHpred* / *EFHreal* ) may provide a better insight on the
effectiveness of a prediction model. Indeed, they suggested that
the use of boxplots of residuals gives a good indication of the
distribution of the error terms and can help to understand the
behavior of the summary statistics [13]. Moreover, they provided
evidence that *MMRE* is a measure of the spread of the variable z,
while *Pred* (0.25) is a measure of how peaked the distribution of z
is. Consequently, they claimed that z is a better measure of
prediction accuracy, since the defined optimum value 1 clearly
indicates whether or not the prediction model underestimates or
overestimates [13], and that boxplots of z are a powerful tool to
evidence it. All these aspects motivated us to analyze the
prediction accuracy of the models by using also the boxplots of
residuals and boxplots of *z*, depicted in Figure 1 and Figure 2,
respectively.

In our case study, these tools largely confirm, in a visual fashion,
the results obtained by considering summary statistics. In
particular, they report that the best results have been obtained
with **Stepwise Regression** applied on Functional Measures, since
its boxplot presents the smallest box length and tails. Moreover,
residuals are symmetrically distributed with median adjacent to

**4.3 Summary Discussion on the Measures**
As for the comparison between the two sets of variables, based on
the analysis of *MMRE*, *MdMRE*, *Pred* (0.25) and of boxplots
residuals and boxplots of z, **Length Measures** have provided the
best results (mainly when using combination of analogy based
estimation and regression tree). Moreover, the main factor
affecting the development effort of dynamic web applications
results the number of Server-Side scripts and Applications
( *SSApp* ). As for Functional Measures, the best results have been
gained with Stepwise regression using as predictor External input.

**5. FINAL REMARKS**

In this paper, we have provided the results of an empirical analysis,
based on an industrial dataset, meant to compare size measures and
modeling techniques for early estimation the web development
effort. In particular, we have focused on Linear Regression,
Regression Tree Analogy-based Estimation and combinations of
these methods, by using as predictors Length Measures and
Functional Measures. The results have highlighted that:

�� Length Measures are best indicators of effort development.
In particular the number of Server-Side scripts/Applications
is the most influencing factor.

�� The combination of Regression Tree and Analogy-based
Estimation has provided the best results in defining an effort
prediction model, if using Length Measures.

�� Stepwise Regression has provided the best results in
defining an effort prediction model when using Functional
Measures (in particular External input).

It is worth reporting that several empirical studies have been carried
out so far to analyze the effectiveness of the considered modeling
techniques in the case of desktop applications. In particular, Briand
*et al.* applied Linear (Stepwise) Regression, Regression Tree and
Analogy-based Estimation, using 1 and 2 analogies, and
combinations of these techniques ([5], [6]). Their results point out
that LR and RT are better than AE in predicting efforts, and in
general RT provides the best results in terms of *MMRE* and
*Pred* (0.25). Our results confirm, in the context of the web
applications, their observations except for the application of AE.
Indeed, while Briand *et al* . achieved weak results, in our cases the
values of *MMRE*, *MdMRE*, and *Pred* (0.25) encourage us to apply
this technique to estimate web application development effort.

Mendes *et al.* also applied Stepwise Regression, Regression Tree
and Analogy-Based Estimation to web hypermedia application

[19]. As for AE they considered three similarity measures, three
choices for number of analogies (1, 2, and 3), and three choices
for the analogy adaptation (mean of k analogies, inverse rank
weighted mean, and median of k analogies). The best result was
achieved by using weighted distance as similarity measure and 1
analogy. Moreover, in another work on the analysis of the AE,
Mendes and Mosley proved that the use of adaptation rules
presented statistically better prediction accuracy than their
counterparts than did not use adaptation [21]. In our case study
the best results with AE have been achieved using 3 (for Length
Measures) and 2 analogies (for Functional Measures) and the
inverse distance weighted mean as analogy adaptation strategy.
Moreover, our analysis confirms Mendes and Mosley's


observation of that Stepwise Regression has a better performance
with respect to Analogy-Based Estimation. Finally, it is worth
pointing out that our results agree with those provided by Mendes
*et al* . in [19], where *MMRE* and *Pred* (0.25) indicated that Linear
Regression models gave better results than Regression Tree
models. However, one of the limitations of the research proposed
in the present paper concerns with the size of the considered
dataset. This was composed by 15 projects, a quite small number
from a statistical point of view. In order to conduct further
researches on wider datasets, we are currently gathering
information on other industrial projects. Moreover another
important aspect to analyze is related to the generalization of the
results in other contexts. Indeed, our data come from a single
company. On one hand, this can lead to more accurate results,
since the data collection task can be carried out in a more

controlled fashion than in a cross-company scenario, as
recognized by Mendes et al. in [20]. But, on the other hand, the
obtained results might not hold in other industrial contexts. In
particular, the models we provided might not supply accurate
predictions when used in different companies /domains, where
some recalibrations could be necessary [20]. As other future work,
other parameters and/or adaptation strategies should be employed
to further investigate Analogy-Based Estimation.
