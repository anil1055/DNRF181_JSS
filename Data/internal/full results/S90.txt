Published online: 29 July 2010

Abstract Support Vector Regression (SVR) is a new generation of Machine Learning
algorithms, suitable for predictive data modeling problems. The objective of this paper is
twofold: first, to investigate the effectiveness of SVR for Web effort estimation using a
cross-company dataset; second, to compare different SVR configurations looking at the one
that presents the best performance. In particular, we took into account three variables’
preprocessing strategies (no-preprocessing, normalization, and logarithmic), in combination
with two different dependent variables (effort and inverse effort). As a result, SVR was
applied using six different data configurations. Moreover, to understand the suitability of
kernel functions to handle non-linear problems, SVR was applied without a kernel, and in
combination with the Radial Basis Function (RBF) and the Polynomial kernels, thus
obtaining 18 different SVR configurations. To identify, for each configuration, which were
the best values for each of the parameters we defined a procedure based on a leave-one-out
cross-validation approach. The dataset employed was the Tukutuku database, which has
been adopted in many previous Web effort estimation studies. Three different training and
test set splits were used, including respectively 130 and 65 projects. The SVR-based
predictions were also benchmarked against predictions obtained using Manual StepWise

1 Introduction

In this paper, we analyze the use of Support Vector Regression (SVR) for Web development
effort estimation using a cross-company dataset. SVR is an approach based on Support Vector
Machines, which are a new generation of Machine Learning algorithms (Vapnik and Lerner
1963). To the best of our knowledge, only four previous studies (Braga et al. 2007, 2008;
Corazza et al. 2009; Oliveira 2006) have investigated the application of SVR for effort
estimation all reporting encouraging results. In particular, (Braga et al. 2007, 2008; Oliveira
2006) focused on software effort estimation using single company datasets and showed that
SVR performed very well also with respect to other techniques, such as linear regression and
various AI-based prediction techniques. The use of SVR for Web effort estimation using a
cross-company dataset was investigated in (Corazza et al. 2009), where predictions given by
SVR were compared to predictions obtained using Manual Stepwise Regression, Case-Based
Reasoning, Bayesian Networks, median and mean effort. Here the results were mixed as SVR
presented overall significantly superior predictions when based on one of the training sets;
however the trend was not the same when using a different training set, thus suggesting for
further analysis. In this paper, we extend the research presented in (Corazza et al. 2009) by:

1. Improving the approach to identify the parameters’ settings used with SVR;
2. Assessing the proposed SVR framework using a third dataset split;
3. Detailing further the description of the SVR framework investigated, the empirical
investigation; and the related work discussion.

As in (Corazza et al. 2009), we employed data on 195 Web projects from the Tukutuku
database, which has been used in numerous Web effort estimation studies (Kitchenham and
Mendes 2004; Kitchenham et al. 2006, 2007; Mendes 2008; Mendes and Kitchenham
2004; Mendes et al. 2002, 2008). The data has been volunteered by Web companies
worldwide, using different technologies, methodologies, and application size. Each project
in the Tukutuku database is characterized using 25 features.
Since we were applying SVR to a cross-company dataset, there were some issues that we
had to take into account. For example, to reduce the risk that large differences in the features’
value ranges in the dataset (usual of cross-company ones) could have unwanted effects on the
predictions, two preprocessing strategies were applied to the features, namely normalization
and logarithmic. In addition, based on the observation that SVR chooses the flattest solution
amongst all the possible ones fitting some constraints, we were interested in assessing whether
or not SVR’s performance could be affected by considering as dependent variables effort or its
inverse. The problems considered in these two cases are equivalent; however the flattest
solution of one case corresponds to the steepest one of the other, and vice-versa.
Summarizing, SVR was applied using six different data configurations, obtained by the
Cartesian product of three different strategies for input data (no preprocessing,
normalization of the features, logarithmic transformation of the features), with two different
dependent variables, namely effort and inverse effort.

Moreover, we analyzed if and how the SVR performance could be improved by
manipulating the project characteristics through the use of kernel functions, which allow for
a mapping from non-linear input data to a new feature space, where the regression
algorithm was used. Among the existing ones, we applied the Polynomial and the Radial
Basis Function (RBF) kernels, which are the most widely adopted in the literature
(Schölkopf and Smola 2002), and compared the results with the ones obtained without
using kernels. These three strategies were applied for each of the six data configurations
previously described, leading to a total of 18 different configurations for SVR. These
configurations were also used in (Corazza et al. 2009). In the present paper we deepened the
analysis by addressing also another key point, namely the setting of the parameters. Indeed, the
application of SVR requires that user provides values for some of the parameters, where some
of them relate to the method and others relate to the specific kernel (if used). Since the choice of
values can have a significant impact on the effectiveness of the technique, herein we searched
for the best parameters’ settings for each configuration using a semi-automatic approach based
on an inner leave-one out cross-validation on the training set.
In conclusion, in this research the effectiveness of SVR for Web effort estimation was
assessed by performing an empirical study, whose research goals were to:

1. investigate how the choice of data preprocessing and kernel used in conjunction with
SVR affects the accuracy of the produced estimates, when employing cross-company
datasets of Web projects;
2. compare the accuracy of the estimates provided by the proposed SVR framework with
the accuracy of the estimates supplied by widely used estimation methods, when
employing cross-company datasets of Web projects.

To address these research goals we performed a hold-out cross-validation, using three
different training sets, each containing data on 130 projects randomly selected from the Tukutuku
database, and test sets, each containing the remaining 65 projects. Thus, for the first research goal
we compared the SVR predictions obtained by all the considered SVR configurations.
As for the second research goal, the best predictions of SVR were compared with the
ones obtained by Manual StepWise Regression (MSWR) and Case-Based Reasoning
(CBR), due to their frequent use in Web and software effort estimation studies, and with the
Mean effort and the Median effort of the training sets.
Prediction accuracy was measured using the Mean Magnitude of Relative Error
(MMRE) (Conte et al. 1986), the Median Magnitude of Relative Error (MdMRE),
Prediction at level l, where l=25 (Pred(25)) (Conte et al. 1986), the Mean Magnitude of
Relative Error relative to the Estimate (MEMRE) (Kitchenham and Mendes 2004), the
Median Magnitude of Relative Error relative to the Estimate (MdEMRE) (Kitchenham and
Mendes 2004), boxplots of absolute residuals (actual effort – estimated effort) and finally
boxplots of z (estimated effort/actual effort). Accuracy of estimates was also assessed by
statistical significance tests, in order to verify if the predictions obtained with one technique
significantly outperformed those of the others.

we used for our empirical study. Section 3 details the main features of the SVR approach,
highlighting the main issues that were addressed in this paper in order to apply this

addition we also detail how the parameters were set. In Section 4 we provide the other
aspects of our empirical study, in particular present the null hypotheses, the validation
method and the evaluation criteria employed. Section 5 discusses our results and
compares them with the ones obtained with other widely adopted estimation techniques.

2 Dataset Description

The data used in this paper came from the Tukutuku database (Mendes et al. 2002). This
database is part of the Tukutuku project, which aims to gather data from completed Web
projects, to develop Web cost estimation models and to benchmark productivity across and
within Web Companies. The Tukutuku database includes information on Web hypermedia
systems and Web applications (Christodoulou et al. 2000). The former are characterized by
the authoring of information using nodes (chunks of information), links (relations between
nodes), anchors, access structures (for navigation) and its delivery over the Web. In
addition, typical developers are writers, artists, and organizations that wish to publish
information on the Web and/or CD-ROMs without the need to use programming languages
such as Java. Conversely, the latter represent software applications that depend on the Web
or use the Web's infrastructure for execution and are characterized by functionality affecting
the state of the underlying business logic. Web applications usually include tools suited to
handle persistent data, such as local file system, (remote) databases, or Web Services.
Typical developers are young programmers fresh from a Computer Science or Software
Engineering degree, managed by more senior staff.

based on collected data.

3.1 Background

SVR is a regression technique based on Support Vector Machines (SVMs) (Vapnik
1998), a very effective and mathematically well founded machine learning approach.
While the SVMs idea can be connected to the Generalized Portrait algorithm developed in
Russia in the sixties, the actual mathematical formulation is inserted in the statistical
machine learning theory developed by Vapnik and Chervonenkis (Vapnik and Chervonenkis 1964).
One of the first applications of SVMs was in the ‘90s at the AT&T Bell Laboratories, for
OCR (Optical Character Recognition) applications. Since then, SVMs have been
successfully applied to a number of different learning problems. In general, when SVM
is used for binary classification, it looks for the hyperplane which separates the elements of
the two considered classes with the largest margin. This goal is attained by solving a
problem of constrained optimization, where a different constraint is introduced for each of
the labeled training set points. However, in some cases, it is not possible to find a

defined a modified version of the approach, able to deal with this case, thanks to the
introduction of slack variables ζ aimed at relaxing the constraint for some of the points.
Indeed, each constraint is “softened” by a slack variable ζ i while a cost C weights the sum
of these constraint violations: this version is usually referred to as soft margin SVM. In
conclusion, a constant C (cost) is introduced to account for the tradeoff between the
optimality of the function and the amount of error introduced by slack variables. The
solution of this optimization problem can be done in the primary or in the dual space,
depending on whether the number of dimensions is smaller or larger than the number of
training examples. The solution of the dual problem, with the introduction of Lagrange
multipliers, shows how the solution can be expressed as a linear combination of some of the
training examples, namely the support vectors; the complexity of the function representation
only depends on their number.
At the same time, the introduction of Lagrange multipliers and dual space solution
makes also the adoption of kernel functions (Hofmann et al. 2008) particularly smooth,
offering a very effective way to face non-linearity. Indeed, a kernel function corresponds to
a dot product in the feature space, where the number of features can be much larger than the

of features increases, the problem is more likely to be linearly separable. Therefore, with the
kernel trick, a linear classifier such as those based on SVMs can be applied also to nonlinear problems.
About the kernel functions, there are two possible solutions: to define an ad-hoc function,
suitable for a specific dataset/problem, or to use kernels of more general application. Among
these latter, the Polynomial and the Radial Basis Function (RBF) such as the Gaussian, and
Sigmoid, such as the Hyperbolic Tangent, are the most widely adopted.
When dealing with a regression problem rather than a classification one, we consider the
application of the support vector approach to function estimation. In this case, the technique
is known as Support Vector Regression (SVR). Indeed, SVR aims at using an approach
based on support vectors to find a function which emulates the training set points with an
error on each point lower than a constant, usually known as ε. Among all possible functions
satisfying such a constraint, usually SVR implementations (including the one we used)
select the flattest one. As SVM used for classification, a soft margin version of the
algorithm can be used, where a constant C weights the errors larger than ε. A graphical
representation is shown in Fig. 1.
A good introduction to SVM/R’s mathematical background, some extensions,
implementations, and a list of references are given in (Schölkopf and Smola 2002).
Pointers to papers about SVR’s history can also be found in the References Section of the
cited tutorial.

3.2 SVR for Effort Estimation

To apply support vector techniques in the field of effort estimation, some preliminary
considerations are needed. To avoid confusion, henceforth we will adopt the terms “feature
space” to identify the kernel functions’ co-domain and “input space” for the original training set.
First of all, in effort estimation the input space consists of the attributes quantifying the
cost drivers for software projects and the target function is an effort estimate. Thus, it turns
out to be a regression problem, and we will deal with SVR and not with SVM.
To apply SVR to effort estimation, the two aspects detailed below had to be carefully
considered:

1) Large differences in the ranges of the features’ values can have the unwanted effect of
giving greater importance to some characteristics than to others. To address this issue, a
data preprocessing step could be applied.

2) Usually data in the input space are non-linear, and thus kernel functions should be
considered to map a problem in a feature space where the target function consists of a
line.

Given these two aspects, they can be addressed using many different solutions. Herein
we considered several strategies and analyzed them to understand the better option in the
specific field of effort estimation with cross-company datasets. In the following
subsections, we describe these strategies.

3.2.1 Data preprocessing

Regarding the ranges of features’ values in the input space, we considered two possible
strategies:

1) features normalization;
2) logarithmic preprocessing.

The first approach consists in considering each feature vector separately and dividing
each of its elements by the vectors’ Euclidian norm, in order to force all vectors to have
norm equal to one [1] .
In relation to the second approach, logarithmic preprocessing, it is usually adopted to
reduce ranges, since it compresses all the values. This transformation can be helpful also
to tackle the linearity issue. Indeed, when a linearity test gives a negative outcome,
meaning that a linear regression cannot be applied, the logarithm is usually the one
applied to raise the probability that the transformed data will fit a linear function (Briand
et al. 2000; Costagliola et al. 2006; Di Martino et al. 2007; Kitchenham and Mendes 2004).
However, note that the attributes describing cost drivers for a software project are greater or
equal to zero. Thus, to obtain positive or null transformed data, we must add 1 to all inputs
before the application of the natural logarithm. In this way, all the quantities that were null
before the transformation are null also afterwards, and all the non null quantities are greater than
zero. Both operations only require the knowledge of the vector describing a single project. This
means that the transformation can be easily applied also to a new project for which effort is to be
estimated.

Another aspect that needed caution was the fact that, among all the different
solutions, SVR chooses the flattest. This could not be necessarily the best choice when
dealing with effort estimation, since, from a managerial point of view, the flattest
solution is the one that underestimates effort the most. To test whether the opposite
choice, i.e., selecting the steepest option, could better suit the estimation problem, we
considered also another preprocessing choice, where the goal function is the inverse of
effort (1/EFH). Indeed, the problems considered with and without the inverse are totally
equivalent, except that the flattest solution of one case corresponds to the steepest one of
the other, and vice-versa.
Summarizing, SVR was applied using six different arrangements of data (named data
configurations in the following), namely normalized features and both unprocessed and
inverse effort; features in logarithmic scale with unprocessed effort, inverse effort,
logarithm of effort and inverse of its logarithm. They are summarized in Table 4.

3.2.2 Kernel Application and Parameters Setting

Within the context of this work we were also interested in understanding the effect of the
application of kernel functions upon each of the previously described data configurations.

vector u, representing the input, only appears in dot products with some other vector v. In
this case, the kernel K(u,v) is used in place of the dot product u · v. As stated before, a
kernel function K(u,v) allows to face the non-linearity of the data (further details can be
found in (Smola and Schölkopf 2004)). We chose to apply the Polynomial and the RBF
kernels as follows, since they are widely used and are implemented in the SVM-light

Finally, the actual application of SVR requires also the choice of values for the

1) parameters of the support vector algorithm, namely ε and C, and
2) kernel parameters, which are specific of each kernel, namely s, c0 and degree for the
Polynomial and γ for the RBF.

The choice of parameters can have a strong impact on the application of the SVR
technique. Therefore, in our study we searched for the best parameter settings using a semiautomatic approach that allowed us to explore a wide range of variables’ values (employing
various nested cycles with small incremental steps). For each run, depending on the kernel,
the number of executions ranged from a few dozens to about 4,000 executions.

4 Null Hypotheses, Validation Method, and Evaluation Criteria

In this section, we present other aspects of the design of our empirical study. In particular,
we formulate the null hypotheses to address the research goals pointed out in the
introduction Section, describe the validation method adopted and the applied evaluation
criteria.

4.1 Null Hypotheses

To address the first research goal, we took into account all the 18 configurations for SVR,
as described in the previous Section. In particular, we formulated the following null
hypothesis:

Hn0: All the SVR data configurations and kernels provide estimates that are not
significantly different.

When the null hypothesis can be rejected with relatively high confidence it is possible to
formulate an alternative hypothesis:

Ha0: Among the considered combinations of data configurations and kernels there is
one that allows SVR to provide estimates (significantly) better than the others.

To address our second research goal, we compared the best predictions achieved by SVR
with the (best) ones obtained by two widely used estimation methods, i.e., the Manual
StepWise Regression (MSWR) (Mendes and Kitchenham 2004) and the Case-Based
Reasoning (CBR) (Shepperd and Kadoda 2001). This allowed us to understand if SVR
performed better than these two widely used prediction techniques. In the Appendix, we
will summarize the key aspects of these methods.
Moreover, we also checked whether the estimates obtained with SVR were significantly
better than predictions obtained by simply using the mean and median of efforts of the
projects in the training sets. This was done because, as suggested by Mendes and
Kitchenham in (Kitchenham and Mendes 2004; Mendes and Kitchenham 2004), if an
estimation technique does not outperform the results achieved by using the mean or median
effort, it cannot be transferred to industry since there would be no value in dealing with
complex computations of estimation methods to predict development effort compared to
simply using as estimate the mean or the median effort of its own past projects.

Empir Software Eng (2011) 16:211–243 221

Thus, regarding our second research goal we formulated the other four null hypotheses:

Hn1: SVR (using the best combination of data configuration and kernel) does not
provide better estimations than MSWR;
Hn2: SVR (using the best combination of data configuration and kernel) does not
provide better estimations than CBR;
Hn3: SVR (using the best combination of data configuration and kernel) does not
provide better estimations than mean of effort of past projects.
Hn4: SVR (using the best combination of data configuration and kernel) does not
provide better estimations than median of effort of past projects.

The related alternative hypotheses can be easily derived.

4.2 Validation Method

To assess the effectiveness of the considered estimation techniques in predicting
development effort, we adopted a hold-out cross-validation method. According to this
approach, the original sample is randomly split into two distinct sets, namely training
and test sets. The model learned/constructed using the training set is used to predict the
dependent variable of the elements in the test set. The errors from applying this model
are accumulated using summary statistic measures (described in the following
subsection).
Let us note that using only a single training and test set split, even if randomly selected,
can provide misleading results, since by chance it could correspond to either an extremely
advantageous or disadvantageous splitting of data for a particular model. Therefore, in our
analysis, we used three random splits, to reduce the probability of having such extreme
cases in the experiments.
In order to compare the results obtained using SVR with other effort estimation
techniques, such as MSWR and CBR, two of the three splits used herein were the same
ones previously employed in (Mendes 2008) and in our previous study (Corazza et al.
2009). In particular, each training set included 130 observations from the original 195
projects contained in the Tukutuku database, while the corresponding test set included the
remaining 65 observations. The third split (not used in (Corazza et al. 2009; Mendes 2008))
was obtained with the same procedure by randomly selecting 130 observations for the
training set and retaining the corresponding 65 observations in the test set.

Finally, to verify if the differences observed using the above measures were legitimate or
due to chance, we checked if the absolute residuals obtained with the application of the
various estimation techniques come from the same population (i.e., to verify the following
null hypothesis: “the two considered populations have identical distributions”) (Kitchenham
et al. 2001; Mendes et al. 2003c). If they do, it means that there are no significant
differences between the data values being compared. We accomplished the statistical
significance test using the nonparametric Wilcoxon Signed Ranks one (Conover 1998). A
one-tailed paired t-test can be used as alternative to the Wilcoxon test (Conover 1998).
However, we used the Wilcoxon test since it is resilient to strong departures from the t-test
assumptions (Briand et al. 2005). The achieved results were intended as statistically
significant at α=0.05. This means that if the derived p-value is less than 0.05, it can be
concluded that the null hypothesis can be rejected.

6 Threats to Validity

Several factors can bias the validity of empirical studies. Here we consider three types of
validity threats: Construct validity, related to the agreement between a theoretical concept
and a specific measuring device or procedure; Conclusion validity, related to the ability to
draw statistically correct conclusions; External validity, related to the ability to generalise
the achieved results.

As highlighted by Kitchenham et al. (Kitchenham et al. 1995), in order to satisfy
construct validity a study has “to establish correct operational measures for the concepts
being studied”. The size measures and cost drivers used in the Tukutuku database and
therefore in our study have been obtained from the results of a survey investigation

(Mendes et al. 2003b), using data from 133 on-line Web forms aimed at giving quotes on
Web development projects. In addition, these measures and cost drivers have also been
confirmed by an established Web company and a second survey involving 33 Web
companies in New Zealand. Consequently, it is our belief that the variables identified are
measures that are meaningful to Web companies and are constructed from information their
customers can provide at a very early stage in the project development.
As for data quality, in order to identify effort guesstimates from more accurate effort
data, companies were asked on how their effort data was collected (see Table 18). At least
for 93.8% of Web projects in the Tukutuku database, effort values were based on more than
just guesstimates.
In relation to the conclusion validity we carefully applied the statistical tests, verifying
all the required assumptions. Moreover, we used a medium size dataset in order to mitigate
the threats related to the number of observations composing the dataset.
About external validity, the dataset used in this investigation comprises data on projects

7 Related Work

Regarding the use of SVR for effort estimation, we are aware of only few previous studies
that investigated the application of this technique for effort estimation. Oliveira (Oliveira
2006), who was the first to apply SVR to this field, used data from 18 applications from the
well-known NASA software project dataset (Bailey and Basili 1981). By using a leave-oneout cross-validation, the author reported that SVR significantly outperformed both linear
regression and Radial Basis Function Networks (RBFNs), in terms of the indicators MMRE
and Pred (25). In a second study (Braga et al. 2007), the authors proposed a method based
on machine learning that provided an effort estimate and corresponding confidence interval.
In particular, they employed robust confidence intervals, which are not associated with the
probability distributions of the errors in the training set. To assess the method, they
performed a case study using the Desharnais (Desharnais 1989) and NASA (Bailey and
Basili 1981) datasets. The results of this empirical analysis showed that the proposed
method was characterized by better performance with respect to the previous study. More
recently the same authors combined the use of genetic algorithms with Support Vector
Regression (SVR), building a regression model employed to predict effort of novel projects
on the basis of historical data (Braga et al. 2008). In particular, they exploited a Genetic
Algorithm (GA) previously used to solve classification problems (Huang and Wang 2006)
to address the problems of feature selection and SVR parameters optimization aiming to
obtain better software effort estimations. The Desharnais and NASA datasets were also

when using a different training set, thus suggesting for further analysis. In this paper, we
have more carefully considered the issue of parameters’ setting and showed that it can
strongly affect the performance of the method. The proposed approach has been assessed
using another dataset split besides the two splits employed in (Corazza et al. 2009) showing
that the predictions obtained with SVR were significantly better than those obtained with all
the other techniques on the three test sets. It is worth noting that with respect to (Conte et al.
1986), in this paper we did not report on the use of the Sigmoid kernel since it provided
always the worse results and for the same reason we did not employ the Bayesian Networks
as benchmarking technique.
In the field of Web effort estimation, several studies have been conducted so far to
investigate and compare the effectiveness of size measures and techniques such as linear
regression (LR), stepwise regression (SWR), case-based reasoning (CBR), and regression
trees (RT) (Cortes and Vapnik 1995; Di Martino et al. 2007; Mendes et al. 2002). In
particular, some length measures (e.g., number of Web pages, number of media, number of
link, etc.) were compared with COSMIC in (Mendes et al. 2002). However, the empirical
results revealed that “none of the obtained models produced reasonable accurate estimates
of the effort”, and “the models did not produce significantly different residual values”. Two
sets of size measures were compared in (Cortes and Vapnik 1995), the first included some
length measures (e.g., number of Web pages, number of server-side script and applications,
etc.) while the second contained the components used to evaluate the Web Objects measure.
The results reported in (Cortes and Vapnik 1995) revealed that the first set provided better

estimates when using CBR while the second set provided better estimates when using LR.
However, the analysis also suggested that there were no significant differences in the
estimations and the residuals obtained with the two sets of measures. In (Di Martino et al.
2007) the following sets of measures were compared: the two sets used in (Cortes and
Vapnik 1995), and the Tukutuku measures (Mendes et al. 2005a). The empirical results
showed that all the measures provided good estimations in terms of MMRE, MdMRE, and
Pred(25) statistics and the study largely confirmed the results of previous works. It is worth
noting that the study presented in (Mendes et al. 2002) employed 37 Web applications
developed by postgraduate students while the studies reported in (Cortes and Vapnik 1995;
Di Martino et al. 2007) were based on 15 industrial Web applications. Recently, Mendes
and Mosley investigated the use of Bayesian Networks (BNs) for Web effort estimation
using the Web applications of the Tukutuku database (Mendes 2008; Mendes and Mosley
2008). In particular, they built eight BNs by exploiting both automatic tools, such as Hugin
and PowerSoft, and a causal graph elicited by a domain expert with parameters
automatically fit using the same training sets used in the automated elicitation (thus
working as composite models ) (Chulani et al. 1999). As in our study, they employed the
Tukutuku database containing data on 195 Web projects and compared the accuracy of the
obtained estimates with those obtained using MSWR, CBR, median and mean effort. That
analysis revealed that MSWR provided significantly better estimations than any of the
models obtained using BNs and was the only approach that provided significantly better
results than the median effort model. Furthermore, the results showed that simpler models,
such as the median effort, can provide better estimates than more complex models, such as
those obtained with BNs (Mendes and Mosley 2008). For these reasons, in the current study
we did not consider BNs. Moreover, to assess the effectiveness of SVR we used also the
two training sets and tests employed in (Mendes and Mosley 2008) thus allowing us to
compare the achieved results with those obtained with MSWR, CBR, median and mean
effort and given in (Mendes and Mosley 2008).

8 Conclusions

In the paper, we investigated the effectiveness of Support Vector Regression for Web effort
estimation using a cross-company dataset. In particular, we explored and compared
different configurations of the method given by the combination of three data preprocessing, two dependent variables, and three kernels (Linear, Polynomial, and RBF). For the
empirical analysis we exploited three different splits of the Tukutuku database into training
and test sets.

The analysis showed that different preprocessing strategies and kernels can significantly
affect the prediction effectiveness of the SVR approach. Moreover, it showed that
configuration C3(R) consisting in Logarithmic preprocessing in combination with the
RBF kernel, provided the best results.
Finally, the predictions obtained with SVR were significantly better than those obtained
with all the other techniques (Manual StepWise Regression and Case-Based Reasoning) on
the three test sets and we can affirm that SVR has proven to be the best technique, among
the ones we considered, for predicting the development effort on the considered crosscompany dataset.
Of course, the experimental results here presented hold only with respect to the
Tukutuku database and they should be assessed on further data as soon as they are
available. However, they are surely interesting enough to suggest the use of SVR among the

238 Empir Software Eng (2011) 16:211–243

techniques that are suitable for Web development effort estimation using a cross-company
dataset.

Another key point to be investigated is related to the parameters’ setting that can have a
significant impact on the effectiveness of the technique. The results reported in the paper
rely on an approach that searched for the best parameters’ settings for each configuration
using a semi-automatic approach based on an inner leave-one out cross-validation on the
training set. Such an approach is characterized by huge computational needs. The use of
suitable heuristics could be investigated to address this issue. Moreover, other evaluation
criteria could be exploited to select the best parameters’ setting in combination with the
other summary measures already adopted.
It would be also useful to understand if the better performance of configuration C3(R)
with respect to the others we have investigated depends on the characteristics of the
employed dataset. In that case, it would be worth to identify the characteristics of the
datasets that make them more suitable for a specific configuration with the aim to design a
Recommender System that could adopt the best configurations for SVR based on the
features of the specific dataset.

Manual Stepwise Regression

Stepwise Regression (Maxwell 2002) is a statistical technique whereby a prediction model
(Equation) is built and represents the relationship between independent (e.g. number of
Web pages) and dependent variables (e.g. total Effort). This technique builds the model by
adding, at each stage, the independent variable with the highest association to the dependent
variable, taking into account all variables currently in the model. It aims to find the set of
independent variables (predictors) that best explains the variation in the dependent variable
(response). In particular, we applied a Manual Stepwise Regression using the technique
proposed by Kitchenham (Kitchenham 1998). Basically the idea is to use this technique to
select the important independent variables, and then to use linear regression to obtain the
final model.

In our study we employed the variables shown in Table 1 with Manual Stepwise
Regression in order to select the most important size measures. Once selected they were the
ones used for cross-validation on each split, i.e. we did not perform a separate Manual
Stepwise Regression for each split; we simply performed a regression using the variables
previously selected using the manual stepwise procedure.
Whenever variables were highly skewed they were transformed before being used in the
forward stepwise procedure. This was done in order to comply with the assumptions
underlying stepwise regression (Maxwell 2002) (i.e. residuals should be independent and
normally distributed; relationship between dependent and independent variables should be
linear). The transformation employed was to take the natural log (Ln), which makes larger
values smaller and brings the data values closer to each other (Maxwell 2002). A new
variable containing the transformed values was created for each original variable that
needed to be transformed.

In addition, whenever a variable needed to be transformed but had zero values, the
natural logarithmic transformation was applied to the variable’s value after adding 1.

To verify the stability of each effort model built using forward stepwise regression, the
following steps were employed (Kitchenham and Mendes 2004):

–
Use of a residual plot showing residuals vs. fitted values to investigate if the residuals
are randomly and normally distributed.
– Calculate Cook’s distance values (Cook 1977) for all projects to identify influential
data points. Any projects with distances higher than 3×(4/n), where n represents the
total number of projects, are immediately removed from the data analysis (Maxwell
2002). Those with distances higher than 4/n but smaller than (3×(4/n)) are removed in
order to test the model stability, by observing the effect of their removal on the model.
If the model coefficients remain stable and the adjusted R [2] (goodness of fit) improves,
the highly influential projects are retained in the data analysis.

Case-Based Reasoning

Case-Based Reasoning (CBR) is a branch of Artificial Intelligence where knowledge of
similar past cases is used to solve new cases (Shepperd & Kadoda 2001). Within the
context of our investigation, the idea behind the use of the CBR technique is to predict the
effort of a new project by considering similar projects previously developed. In particular,
the completed projects are characterized in terms of a set of p features and form the case
base. The new project is also characterized in terms of the same p features and it is referred
as the target case. Then, the similarity between the target case and the other cases in the pdimensional feature space is measured, and the most similar cases are used, possibly with
adaptations to obtain a prediction for the target case. To apply the method, we have to select
the relevant project features, the appropriate similarity function, the number of analogies to
choose the similar projects to consider for estimation, and the analogy adaptation strategy
for generating the estimation. The selection of the similarity function and the number of
analogies are crucial decisions. The similarity measure used in this study is the Euclidean
distance as this has been the measure used in the literature with the best results (Mendes et al.
2003c). In addition, all the project attributes considered by the similarity function had equal
influence upon the selection of the most similar project(s).
Estimates were based on the average effort of the most similar projects in the case base,
with no different weights for attributes or adaptation of the estimated effort.
