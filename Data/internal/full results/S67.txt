Abstract

In 2004 [Kitchenham, B.A., Mendes, E., 2004a. Software productivity measurement using multiple size measures. IEEE Transactions
on Software Engineering 30 (12), 1023–1035, Kitchenham, B.A., Mendes, E., 2004b. A comparison of cross-company and singlecompany effort estimation models for web applications. In: Proceedings Evaluation and Assessment in Software Engineering (EASE’
04), pp. 47–55] (S1) investigated, using data on 63 Web projects, to what extent a cross-company cost model could be successfully
employed to estimate development effort for single-company Web projects. Their effort models were built using Forward Stepwise
Regression (SWR) and they found that cross-company predictions were significantly worse than single-company predictions. This study
S1 was extended by Mendes and Kitchenham [Mendes, E., Kitchenham, B.A., 2004. Further comparison of cross-company and within
company effort estimation models for web applications. In: Proceedings International Software Metrics Symposium (METRICS’04),
Chicago, Illinois, September 11–17th, 2004. IEEE Computer Society, pp. 348–357] (S2), who used SWR and Case-based reasoning
(CBR), and data on 67 Web projects from the Tukutuku database. They built two cross-company and one single-company models
and found that both SWR cross-company models and CBR cross-company data provided predictions significantly worse than singlecompany predictions. Since 2004 another 83 projects were volunteered to the Tukutuku database, and recently used by Mendes et al.

[Mendes, E., Di Martino, S., Ferrucci, F., Gravino, C., in press. Effort estimation: How valuable is it for a web company to use a
cross-company data set, compared to using its own single-company data set? In: Proceedings of International World Wide Web
Conference (WWW’07), Banff, Canada, 8–12 May] (S3), who partially replicated Mendes and Kitchenham’s study (S2), using SWR
and CBR. They corroborated some of S2’s findings (SWR cross-company model and the CBR cross-company data provided predictions
significantly worse than single-company predictions) however they replicated only part of S2. The objective of this paper (S4) is therefore
to extend Mendes et al.’s work and fully replicate S2. We used the same dataset used in S3, and our results corroborated most of those
obtained in S2. The main difference between S2 and our study was that one of our SWR cross-company models showed significantly
similar predictions to the single-company model, which contradicts the findings from S2.

dataset) to produce more accurate effort estimates (e.g.

three main problems can occur when relying on single-company data (Briand et al., 2000; Kitchenham and Mendes,

(i) The time required to accumulate enough data on past
projects from a single-company may be prohibitive.
(ii) By the time, the dataset is large to be of use, technologies used by the company may have changed, and
older projects may no longer be representative of current practices.
(iii) Care is necessary as data needs to be collected in a
consistent manner.

These three problems have motivated the use of crosscompany datasets for effort estimation and productivity
benchmarking. The use of a cross-company dataset seems
particularly useful for companies that do not have their
own data on past projects from which to obtain their estimates, or that have data on projects developed in different
application domains and/or technologies. However, the use
of cross-company datasets has problems of its own (Briand
et al., 2000; Kitchenham and Mendes, 2004a,b; Mendes
and Kitchenham, 2004):

(i) Care is necessary as data needs to be collected in a
consistent manner by means of a uniform data collection control across different companies.
(ii) Differences in processes and practices may result in
trends that may differ significantly across companies.
(iii) Projects may need to be partitioned (e.g. according to
their completion dates) to identify those that used
current development practices from those that did

not.
(iv) Project data should represent a random sample representative of a well-defined population (i.e. it is not
sufficient that the dataset is large).

To date, 12 studies (nine in Software engineering and
three in Web engineering) have investigated if estimates
obtained using cross-company datasets can be as accurate
as the ones obtained using single-company datasets (Briand
et al., 1999; Briand et al., 2000; Jeffery et al., 2000; Jeffery
et al., 2001; Kitchenham and Mendes, 2004b; Lefley and
Shepperd, 2003; Lokan and Mendes, 2006; Maxwell

et al., 2005; Wieczorek and Ruhe, 2002; Mendes et al., in
press). Here we focus only on the three studies (S1 – Kitchenham and Mendes, 2004b; S2 – Mendes and Kitchenham,
2004; and S3 – Mendes et al., in press) that used data on
Web projects; however, except for Mendes et al. (in press),
a detailed comparison of all previous studies is provided in
(Kitchenham et al., 2006).
S1, S2, and S3 all investigated to what extent a crosscompany cost model could be successfully employed to estimate effort for single-company Web projects (see Table 1).


All these three studies employed data from the Tukutuku
database (Mendes et al., 2003).
S1 used data on 63 Web projects (50 projects from a
cross-company dataset (S1CCM) and 13 from a singlecompany dataset (Company 1)). Their cross-company
model was based on an analysis of the full dataset, including the 13 single-company projects, where they used the
variables selected when analysing the entire dataset of 63
projects and recalibrated the parameters after removing
the Company 1 data. Their effort models were built using
Forward Stepwise Regression (SWR) and they found that
cross-company predictions were superior to expert-based
and median-based predictions, however significantly worse
than single-company predictions. This study was extended
by S2, who used SWR and Case-based reasoning (CBR),
and data on 67 Web projects from the Tukutuku database,
to investigate similar issues to those previously investigated. Their cross-company dataset combined both the
cross- and single-company datasets used in S1, and their
single-company dataset contained data on 14 Web projects
gathered after S1 had been carried out (Company 2). However, both studies were independent because the singlecompany data used in S2 was not part of the Tukutuku
dataset used in S1. It was collected from a single-company
some time after the first set of data had been collected and
analysed (Kitchenham et al., 2006). They built two crosscompany and one single-company models. One of their
cross-company models (S2CCM1) was completely independent of the single-company dataset, i.e. it was solely based
on the data from the 53 cross-company projects. The other
model (S2CCM2) was based on an analysis of the full data
set including Company 2 data, where they used the variables selected by analysing the full dataset and recalibrated
the parameters after removing the Company 2 data. They
found that cross-company predictions, for both cross-company models, using SWR were significantly worse than single-company, median-based and expert-based predictions;
and CBR cross-company predictions were superior to single-company predictions. They also used the Company 2
model to estimate effort for the cross-company projects,
and found that the predictions obtained were extremely
poor. Recently, S3 partially replicated Mendes and Kitchenham’s study (S2), using SWR and CBR, and data on 83
Web projects volunteered to the Tukutuku database after
S2 was conducted (68 from a cross-company dataset and
15 from a single-company dataset (Company 3 data)). They
built one cross-company and one single-company models.
Their cross-company model was completely independent
of the single-company dataset. They found that cross-company predictions using both SWR and CBR were significantly worse than single-company, median-based and
mean-based predictions. In addition, their single-company
predictions were similar to mean- and median-based predictions. Therefore some of their results contradicted those

from S2.

2. Research method

2.1. Dataset description

The analysis presented in this paper was based on data
coming from 83 Web projects of the Tukutuku database
(Mendes et al., 2003), which aims to collect data about
Web projects, to be used to develop Web cost estimation
models and to benchmark productivity across and within
Web Companies. The Tukutuku includes Web hypermedia
systems and Web applications (Briand et al., 2000). The
former are characterized by the authoring of information
using nodes (chunks of information), links (relations
between nodes), anchors, access structures (for navigation)
and its delivery over the Web. In addition, typical developers are writers, artists and organisations that wish to publish information on the Web and/or CD-ROMs without
the need to use programming languages such as Java. Conversely, the latter represents software applications that
depend on the Web or use the Web’s infrastructure for execution and are characterized by functionality affecting the
state of the underlying business logic. Web applications
usually include tools suited to handle persistent data, such
as local file system, (remote) databases, or Web Services.
The Tukutuku database currently has data on 150 projects where

 - Projects come from 10 different countries, mainly New
Zealand (56%), Brazil (12.7%), Italy (10%), Spain
(8%), United States (4.7%), England (2.7%), and Canada (2%).



 - Project types are new developments (56%) or enhancement projects (44%).

 - The applications are mainly Legacy integration (27%),
Intranet and eCommerce (15%).

 - The languages used are mainly HTML (88%), Javascript
(DHTML/DOM) (76%), PHP (50%), Various Graphics
Tools (39%), ASP (VBScript, .Net) (18%), and Perl
(15%).

2.2.1. Forward stepwise regression
Stepwise regression (Maxwell, 2002) is a statistical technique whereby a prediction model (Equation) is built, and
represents the relationship between independent (e.g. number of Web pages) and dependent variables (e.g. total
Effort). This technique builds the model by adding, at each
stage, the independent variable with the highest association
to the dependent variable, taking into account all variables
currently in the model. It aims to find the set of independent variables (predictors) that best explains the variation
in the dependent variable (response).
In our study we employed the same set of variables used
in S3, shown in Table 3. Whenever variables were highly
skewed they were transformed before being used in the forward stepwise procedure. This was done in order to comply
with the assumptions underlying stepwise regression (Maxwell, 2002) (e.g. residuals should be independent and normally distributed; relationship between dependent and
independent variables should be linear). The transformation employed was to take the natural log (ln), which
makes larger values smaller and brings the data values closer to each other (Maxwell, 2002). A new variable containing the transformed values was created for each original
variable that needed to be transformed. All new variables

are identified as Lvarname, e.g. Lnlang represents the transformed variable nlang. In addition, whenever a variable
needed to be transformed but had zero values, the natural
logarithmic transformation was applied to the variable’s
value after adding 1.

Table 5 contains the variables we considered in the forward stepwise regression procedure. These are the same
variables used in S3 for SCM and S4CCM1. All the

cross-company variables, for both the cross-company models, needed to be transformed since they were not normally
distributed; three of the single-company variables also
needed to be transformed (DevTeam, TeamExp, and
TotEff).

2.2.2. Case-based reasoning
CBR is a branch of Artificial Intelligence where knowledge of similar past cases is used to solve new cases
(Shepperd and Kadoba, 2001). Within the context of
our investigation, the idea behind the use of the CBR
technique is to predict the effort of a new project by considering similar projects previously developed. In particular, completed projects are characterized in terms of a set
of p features (e.g. number of Web pages) and form the
case base. The new project is also characterized in terms
of the same p attributes and it is referred as the target
case. Then, the similarity between the target case and
the other cases in the p-dimensional feature space is measured, and the most similar cases are used, possibly with
adaptations to obtain a prediction for the target case.
To apply the method, we have to select: the relevant project features, the appropriate similarity function, the number of analogies to select the similar projects to consider
for estimation, and the analogy adaptation strategy for
generating the estimation. The selection of the similarity
function and the number of analogies are crucial decisions. Like S2 and S3, the similarity measure used in this
study is the Euclidean distance. In addition, we used all
the original numerical attributes, and all the project attributes had equal influence upon the selection of the most
similar project(s) when considered by the similarity
function.

2.2.3. Prediction accuracy
The accuracy of the effort estimates obtained using
SWR and CBR was measured using the same measures
employed in S3 and S4, that is, the Mean Magnitude of
Relative Error (MMRE), Median MRE, and Prediction
at 25% (Pred(25)) (Conte et al., 1986). In addition we also
employed boxplots of the absolute residuals to visually
compare samples and statistical significance tests to compare samples’ distributions (a = 0.05).
Pred(n) measures the percentage of estimates that are
within n% of the actual values, and n is usually set at
25%. MRE is the basis for calculating MMRE and

3. Steps to follow to answer our research questions

This Section details the steps that need to be carried out
to answer each of the two research questions this study
investigated, by exploiting the dataset, the modelling techniques, and the evaluation criteria described in the previous
two sections. These were the same steps used in S2. Both
questions are also presented to provide a context for each
set of steps.

Question 1. How successful is a cross-company dataset at
estimating effort for projects from a single-company?

Steps to follow:

(1) Apply SWR to fit a baseline cross-company model to
the full dataset of 83 projects. Not applicable to CBR
as this technique does not explicitly build a model.
(2) If the model uses transformed variables, convert the
model back to the raw data scale. Not applicable to
CBR.
(3) Obtain prediction accuracy statistics for the crosscompany baseline model using a leave-one-out
cross-validation. Also applicable to CBR.
(4) Recalibrate the baseline model from step 1 by omitting Company 3 projects. The recalibrated model is
named S4CCM2.
(5) Use the model in step 4 to estimate effort for each of
the 15 single-company projects. The single-company
projects are the validation set used to obtain effort
estimates. The estimated effort obtained for each project is also used to calculate accuracy statistics (e.g.
MRE). Not applicable to CBR.
(6) The overall model accuracy for S4CCM2 is aggregated from the validation set (e.g. MMRE,
MdMRE). Not applicable to CBR.
(7) Apply SWR to build a cross-company model
(S4CCM1) from scratch using only the 68 projects
from the cross-company dataset. Not applicable to
CBR as this technique does not explicitly build a
model.
(8) If the model uses transformed variables, convert the
model back to the raw data scale. Not applicable to
CBR.
(9) Use the model in step 7 to estimate effort for each of
the 15 single-company projects. The single-company
projects are the validation set used to obtain effort
estimates. The estimated effort obtained for each pro

ject is also used to calculate accuracy statistics (e.g.
MRE). The equivalent for CBR is to use the crosscompany dataset as a case base to estimate effort
for each of the single-company projects.
(10) The overall model accuracy for S4CCM1 is aggregated from the validation set (e.g. MMRE, MdMRE).
Also applicable to CBR.

These steps are used to simulate a situation where a
company uses two cross-company models to estimate effort
for its new projects.

Question 2. How successful is a cross-company dataset,
compared to a single-company dataset, for effort estimation?

Steps to follow:

(1) Apply SWR to build a single-company cost model
(S4SCM) using the single-company dataset. Not
applicable to CBR since this technique does not
explicitly build a model.
(2) Obtain the prediction accuracy of estimates for the
model obtained in (1) using a leave-one-out cross-validation. Also applicable to CBR.
(3) The overall model accuracy for S4SCM is aggregated
across the n validation sets. Also applicable to CBR.
(4) Compare the accuracy obtained in Step 3 to that
obtained for S4CCM1 and S4CCM2. Also applicable
to CBR.

Steps 1–3 simulate a situation where a company builds a
model using its own dataset, and then uses this model to
estimate effort for new projects.
In addition to answering the two research question
abovementioned we also used the S4SCM model to predict
effort for the other 68 Web projects and constructed accuracy statistics from the predictions. We also repeated this
step with CBR. This allowed us to assess how good the single-company model would be at predicting another company’s projects.

5. Obtaining effort estimates using case-based reasoning

There is no clear answer to date as to what is the best tech
nique to employ to obtain effort estimates, for a given dataset. Shepperd and Kadoda suggested that dataset
characteristics should have a strong influence on the choice
of techniques to employ to obtain effort estimates (Shepperd
and Kadoba, 2001). The less ‘‘messy’’ the dataset, i.e., small
number of outliers, small amount of collinearity, strong relationship between independent and dependent variables, the
higher the chances that regression analysis will give the best
estimation accuracy. Conversely, very ‘‘messy’’ datasets
should use CBR to obtain more accurate effort estimates.
Since the Tukutuku dataset presents some level of collinearity and outliers, S2 investigated its use to obtain effort
estimates, and S3 repeated some of the steps carried out
by S2, namely:

(i) To use the 68 cross-company projects in the case base
to obtain effort estimates for each of the 15 singlecompany projects. This step corresponds to the use
of the S4CCM1 model and the 15 single-company
projects as validation set.

6. Discussion

The research questions addressed in this study are as
follows:

1. How successful is a cross-company dataset when estimating effort for projects from a single-company, when
the estimate is obtained from a dataset that does not

include that company’s data.
2. How successful is the use of a cross-company dataset,
compared to a single-company dataset.

Summary results for our study for both SWR and CBR
are presented in Table 18, pairing SWR to CBR results.
Our first research question is addressed by the results
from Sections 4.2 and 5.

The accuracy of estimates of both regression-based
cross-company models does not indicate good prediction
accuracy. MMREs and MdMREs for both S4CCM1 and
S4CCM2 are poor (see Table 16) according to a suggested
threshold of 25% to indicate good accuracy (Conte et al.,
1986), and both Pred(25) are extremely poor according to
another suggested threshold of 75% to indicate a good pre

7. Threats to validity

As for the threats to the validity of this study, our comments are as follows:

7.1. Construct validity

The size measures and cost drivers used in the Tukutuku
database and therefore in our study have been obtained
from the results of a survey investigation (Mendes et al.,
2003), using data from 133 on-line Web forms aimed at giving quotes on Web development projects. In addition, these
measures and cost drivers have also been confirmed by an
established Web company and a second survey involving
33 Web companies in New Zealand. Consequently it is
our belief that the 25 variables identified are measures that
are meaningful to Web companies and are constructed
from information their customers can provide at a very
early stage in the project development.
As for the quality of the effort data, we asked companies
how their effort data was collected (see Table 19).
At least for 83% of Web projects in the Tukutuku database effort values were based on more than guesstimates. In
relation to the 83 projects used in this study, 85% of the 68
cross-company projects and 100% of the 15 single-company projects are also more than guesstimates.

7.2. Internal validity

Table 4 shows that Company 3 projects used on average
six different languages, were on average developed by a
team of six people who had an average development experience of a year. Conversely, the cross-company projects

We compared the productivity between Company 3 and
cross-company projects by employing the productivity
method proposed by Kitchenham and Mendes (2004a)
where productivity is measured using the following
Equation:

Productivity ¼ AdjustedSize=Effort: ð10Þ

The AdjustedSize measure contains only size measures that
together are strongly associated with effort. In addition, the
relationship between these size measures and effort does
not need to be linear.

The benefits of using this method for measuring productivity are as follows (Kitchenham and Mendes, 2004a):

 - The standard value of productivity is one, since it is
obtained using the ratio of estimated to actual effort.

 - A productivity value greater than one suggests aboveaverage productivity.

 - A productivity value smaller than one suggests belowaverage productivity.

 - The stepwise regression technique used to build a regression model that represents the AdjustedSize measure can
also be employed to construct upper and lower bounds
on the productivity measure. These bounds can be used
to assess whether the productivity achieved by a specific
project is significantly better or worse than expected.

 - The productivity measure automatically allows for diseconomies (or economies) of scale before being used in
a productivity analysis. This means that an investigation
of factors that affect productivity will only select factors
that affect the productivity of all projects. If we ignore
the impact of diseconomies (or economies) or scale, we
run the risk of detecting factors that differ between large
and small projects rather than factors that affect the productivity of all projects.

Both the independent samples T-test and the Mann–
Whitney Test confirmed that productivity values all came
from the same distribution.

7.3. External validity

The data set used in this investigation comprises data on
projects volunteered by individual companies, and therefore it does not represent a random sample of projects from
a defined population. This means that we cannot conclude
that the results of this study promptly apply to other companies different from the ones that volunteered the data
used here. However, we believe that Web companies that

in this paper may be able to apply our results to their
Web projects.

9. Conclusions

Our results show that the cross-company data when
used to estimate effort for the Company 3 projects provided
poor prediction accuracy. However, unlike the CBR-based
predictions and predictions obtained using the S4CCM1
model, predictions for Company 3 projects based on the
S4CCM2 model were not significantly different (worse)
than the predictions for Company 3 projects obtained using
the S4SCM model. Our results for S4CCM2 contradict

those from S2, however, overall the cross-company predictions for single-company projects were significantly worse
than the single-company predictions for single-company
projects. None of our models, except for the baseline
model, were significantly different from the median or
mean-based effort models. These are similar results to those
obtained in S2, where the only model that presented significantly better predictions than the median-based effort
model was the regression-based single-company model.
One possible reason for the better performance of the single-company dataset, compared to the cross-company one,
may be related to the size of the single-company datasets.
Recently, Kitchenham et al. (2006), by means of a systematic review, found that all studies where single-company
predictions were significantly better than cross-company
predictions employed smaller single-company datasets,
smaller number of projects in the cross-company models,
and databases where maximum effort was also smaller.
They speculate that as single-company datasets grow, they
incorporate less similar projects so that differences between
single- and cross-company datasets cease to be significant.
As part of our future work we aim to replicate our study
using further data.
