***Abstract*** **—The aim of the paper is to investigate to what**
**extend some COSMIC-based approximate countings can be**
**useful for project managers to early estimate effort of Web ap-**
**plications. We considered the number of COSMIC Functional**
**Processes and the Average Functional Process approach pro-**
**posed by the COSMIC method documentation. We carried out**
**an empirical analysis employing data from 25 Web applications**
**to assess whether the two approximate sizes can be exploited**
**to get accurate effort estimations. We compared the obtained**
**estimations with those achieved with baseline benchmarks and**
**employing the standard COSMIC method. The analysis high-**
**lights that the considered approximate countings provide good**
**early estimations, significantly better than those obtained with**
**baseline benchmarks. Moreover, the first counting provides**
**results better than the Average Functional Process approach.**
**The use of COSMIC-based approximate countings is a suitable**
**approach for early effort estimations.**

***Keywords*** **-COSMIC,** **Early** **effort** **estimation,** **Empirical**
**study, Linear regression**

I. I NTRODUCTION

Software development effort estimation concerns estimating the amount of human effort needed to realize a
software system. It is crucial to have accurate effort estimations that can help project managers to plan and monitor
software projects for delivering the product on time and
within budget. Significant over or under-estimations can be
very expensive and deleterious for a company [1]. Thus,
several approaches have been proposed to carry out such an
estimation. Many of them exploit an estimation model that,
given data about project characteristics, are able to derive
an effort estimation. One of the most important data input
into these models is the application size.

Functional Size Measurement (FSM) methods have been
widely applied in software engineering for estimating the
size of software systems [2] [3]. In the context of Web
applications, a few investigations have been performed to
analyze the application of 1 *[st]* generation of FSM method,
i.e., Function Point Analysis (FPA) (e.g., [4] [5] [6]). Some
of them have highlighted difficulties in the application
of FPA suggesting the use other FSM methods for Web
applications, such as COSMIC [6].

COSMIC raised the 2 *[nd]* generation of FSM methods,
due to several distinguishing characteristics: it was the first
FSM method conform to the ISO/IEC14143/1 standard; it is
based on fundamental principles of software engineering and


measurement theory; it is suitable to a wide set of different
software types; and it poses the accent on the movements
of data. Encouraging results in the use of COSMIC for
estimating Web application development effort have been
reported in [8] [9] [10] [11] [12].

In this paper we investigate to what extend some
COSMIC-based approximate countings can be useful for
project managers to early estimate effort of Web applications. Indeed, such approximate counting can be useful it
can happen either early in the life of a project before the
Functional User Requirements (FURs) have been specified
in detail (“early sizing”), or when a measurement is needed
but there is insufficient time or resources to apply the
standard detailed method (“rapid sizing”). These motivations are not mutually exclusive and contribute to reach
a trade-off between a correct measurement and time and

budget available. In particular, we analyze two approximate
countings. The first one is based on the use of the number
of COSMIC Functional Processes while the second one is

the Average Functional Process approach proposed in the
COSMIC documentation [7].

We have carried out an empirical analysis employing
data from 25 Web applications to assess whether the two
COSMIC-based approximate sizes can be exploited to get
accurate effort estimations. To this end, linear regression
analysis is used to build effort estimation models based
on those size measures and the accuracy of the obtained
estimations is assessed by employing leave-one-out cross
validation and widely used summary measures. The obtained
estimations are also compared with those achieved with
baseline benchmarks and the standard COSMIC method.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
II reports on related work, while Section III illustrates the
main aspects of COSMIC. The design of the performed
empirical analysis is described in Section IV. The results
of the analysis and their discussion are presented in Section
V. Threats to validity are discussed in Section VI, while
conclusion and future work conclude the paper.

[10] [11] [12]). To the best to our knowledge no study have

Nevertheless, researches that can be related to our work are

Two approaches for simplifying the application of Function Points Analysis are the Early & Quick Function Points
(EQFP) [13] and the simplified versions of NESMA [14].
The idea behind the application of EQFP is to allow a size
estimation before the requirements analysis is completed.
The idea of the approach is that different parts of the
software can be measured at the different level of details.

Thus, exploiting functional components more coarse-grained
than the BFCs (i.e., EI, EO, EQ, ILF, EIF) of FPA, EQFP
approach produces an approximation of the size in terms
of Function Points. In particular, EQFP classifies data and
processes of an application in several groups according
to a hierarchy [13], where some groups contain the Base
Functional Components (BFCs) of FPA and other groups
are aggregation of Base Functional Processes (e.g., Macro
Process, General Process) or Data Groups (e.g., Multiple
Data Group).

The first approximation suggested by NESMA (named
Indicative NESMA) is based on the identification of Internal
Logical Files (ILF) and External Interface Files (EIF),
i.e., without using the contribution of Transactions Functions (i.e., EI, EQ, and EO) of FPA, and applying predefined weights [14]. The second approximation proposed by
NESMA (named Estimated NESMA) exploits all the BFCs
of FPA, but the complexity of the identified components are
not determined. By default, a low complexity is associated
to ILF and EIF elements while a medium complexity is
considered for EI, EQ, and EO.

EQFP and the two simplified NESMA approaches have
been also applied to size both traditional and real-time
applications for evaluating their accuracy with respect to
FPA [15]. The results of the performed analysis show that
EQFP produced more accurate size estimations for non realtime applications, while NESMA approaches turned out to
be quite good for both applications types.

Related work can also be considered those that have

analyzed the different contribution of BFCs against the
overall fsu value (e.g., UFP for IFPUG [16] or CFP for
COSMIC [17]) in estimating software development effort.
In particular, Abran *et al.* were the first to analyze the
contribution of single BFCs to the development effort of
a project [18]. The results of the performed analysis reveal
that correlation with development effort was different for the
individual BFC types within a data set. Other two empirical
studies based on ISBSG samples [19] showed that linear
regression models employing two or more BFCs (e.g., EI,
EO, EQ, ILF, EIF for the IFPUG method and Entry, eXit,
and Read, Write for the COSMIC method) as independent
variables instead of the whole single fsu value (e.g., UFP for


the IFPUG method or CFP for the COSMIC method) were
characterized by more accurate estimations [20] [21]. The
study presented in [22] and its replication described in [23]
are further applications of the idea proposed in [20] [21],
by exploiting a data set of Web applications developed by
a single company operating in Italy. The results confirmed
that the use of a subset of BFC types provide estimations
of development effort close (i.e., no statistical significant
difference) to the ones achieved the total functional size.

III. COSMIC

COSMIC is a widely adopted method for measuring
software functional size and has been approved as an international standard by ISO (ISO/IEC 19761:2003) [17]. It was
originally conceived for business and real-time applications,
but it can be successfully applied in any other context,
where software is mainly dominated by large amounts of
data movements [24]. Indeed, the basic idea underlying the
COSMIC approach is that, for these kinds of software, the
most of development efforts are devoted to handle data
movements from/to persistent storage and users. Thus, the
number of these movements can provide a meaningful sight
of the system size [24].

COSMIC is composed of a set of models, principles, rules,
and processes applicable to the FURs of a given piece of
software. The first step to obtain the COSMIC measure is
to define the COSMIC Software Context Model. This model
introduces the principles and concepts needed for identifying
the FURs of the piece of software to be measured. Then, the
COSMIC Generic Software Model is applied to the FURs
to identify the components of the functionality that will be
measured. In particular, the Software Context Model allows
us to define why a measurement is required (Purpose) and to
select the set of FURs to be included in a specific functional
size measurement exercise (Scope). Furthermore, Functional
User and Boundary concepts can be specified. A Functional
User is a (type of) user that is a sender and/or an intended
recipient of data in the FURs of a piece of software, while
the Boundary is defined as a conceptual interface between
the software being measured and its functional users. Then,
the concepts of the Generic Software Model are applied to
the FURs, to identify the set of “features” provided by the
software. This model is the key of the COSMIC method,
assuming that ( *i* ) each FUR can be mapped into a unique
functional process, ( *ii* ) each functional process consists of
sub-processes, and ( *iii* ) each sub-process may be either a
data movement or a data manipulation.

A Functional Process is an elementary component of a set
of FURs comprising a unique, cohesive, and independently
executable set of data movements. It is triggered by a data
movement from a functional user that informs the piece of
software that the functional user has identified a triggering
event. A Functional Sub-Process may be a data movement or
a data manipulation. As an approximation for measurement

purposes, data manipulation sub-processes are not separately
measured; the functionality of any data manipulation is
assumed to be accounted for by the data movement with
which it is associated [24]. To identify and measure data
movements other three concepts have to be specified. A
Triggering Event is an event (something that happens) that
causes a functional user of the piece of software to initiate
(trigger) one or more functional processes. A Data Group is
a distinct, non empty, non ordered, and non redundant set
of data attributes describing a complementary aspect of the
same object of interest. A Data Attribute is the smallest piece
of information, within an identified data group, carrying a
meaning from the perspective of the software FURs.

Data movements can be of four types. An Entry (E) moves
a data group from a functional user across the boundary
into the functional process where it is required. An Exit (X)
moves a data group from a functional process across the
boundary to the functional user that requires it. A Read (R)
moves a data group from persistent storage within each of
the functional process that requires it. A Write (W) moves
a data group lying inside a functional process to persistent

storage.

In the measurement phase, the data movements of each
functional process have to be identified and used to obtain a
size measure of the software. Each data movement is counted

as 1 COSMIC Function Point (CFP) that is the COSMIC
measurement standard. Thus, the size of an application
within a defined scope is obtained by summing the sizes of
all the functional processes identified. For more details about
the COSMIC method, readers are referred to the COSMIC
Measurement Manual [24].

IV. E MPIRICAL ANALYSIS : D ESIGN

This section presents the design of the empirical study
we carried out to assess the effectiveness of two COSMIC
based approximate countings for the early estimation of Web
application development effort. It is worth noting that the
assessment of a size measure for effort estimation requires
the use of an estimation technique to build a model relying
on the selected size measure. In our empirical analysis
we applied COSMIC in combination with simple linear
regression (SLR), that is a model based approach, since it
has been widely and successfully employed in the industrial
context and in several researches to estimate development
effort (see e.g., [25], [26], [27], [28]).

In our analysis we considered the number of COSMIC
Functional Processes (CFunP) and the Average Functional
Process (AFP) approach proposed by the COSMIC method
documentation [7]. CFunP consists of determining the number of Function Processes by applying the standard COSMIC
method described in Section III. AFP is the first of the four
approaches suggested by the COSMIC community to obtain


early size estimations of the software [1] . It consists of two
steps. In the first step the functional processes of previously
developed applications are identified. Then, the size for
each functional process is measured in terms of COSMIC
Function Points using the standard COSMIC method. In the
second step, the functional processes for the new application
are determined. Then, the approximate size is calculated
by multiplying the number of functional processes and the
average size obtained in previous step.

Thus, in the case of the first approximate counting CFunP
we build a linear model based on the number of COSMIC

Functional Processes, while in the case of the second approximate counting AFP we build a linear model based on
the number of COSMIC Function Points.

In particular, the research questions we addressed are:

RQ1 Can CFunP and AFP provide good early size estima
tions for Web effort prediction?
RQ2 Are the early effort estimations obtained using CFunP

and AFP statistically better than the estimations obtained using baseline benchmarks?
RQ3 Are the early effort estimations obtained using CFunP

and AFP comparable to the estimations obtained using
the standard COSMIC method?

As done in [29] [30], we employed as baseline benchmarks the estimations obtained by employing the mean of
effort (MeanEFF) and the median of effort (MedEFF) as
predictor of the effort. The aim is to assess whether the
estimations obtained with COSMIC Functional Processes as

size measure are significant better than estimations simply
based on the mean and median effort of the past projects
developed by a software company. Indeed, if the estimations
obtained with mean or median effort are similar to those

obtained with the employed measures, then for a software
company it could be more useful to simply use the mean or
the median effort of the past developed applications to have
an early estimation rather than dealing with more complex
computations of software sizes.

*A. Estimation method*

We applied simple linear regression (SLR) which is a
statistical technique that explores the relationship between
a dependent variable and one independent variables [31],
providing a prediction model described by an equation

*y* = *bx* + *c* (1)

where *y* is the dependent variable, *x* is the independent
variable, *b* is the coefficient that represents the amount
the variable *y* changes when the variables *x* changes 1
unit, and *c* is the intercept. In our empirical study we
exploited SLR to obtain linear regression models that use the

*B. Dataset*

Data for our empirical study were provided by an Italian
medium-sized software company, whose core business is the
development of enterprise information systems, mainly for
local and central government. Among its clients, there are

public institutions. The company is specialized in the design,
development, and management of solutions for Web portals,
enterprise intranet/extranet applications (such as Content

etc.), and Geographical Information Systems. It has about
fifty employees, it is certified ISO 9001:2000, and it is also

This company provided us detailed information on 25
Web applications they developed. In particular, this set
includes e-government, e-banking, Web portals, and Intranet

*C. Validation method and evaluation criteria*

In our analysis, we carried out a validation to verify
whether or not the obtained effort predictions are useful
estimations of the actual development effort. To this end, we
applied a cross-validation, that is the splitting of a dataset
into training and validation sets. Training sets are used to
build models with SLR and validation sets are used to

validate the obtained models. In particular, we exploited a
leave-one-out cross validation, which means that the original
dataset is divided into *n=25* different subsets (25 is the size
of the original dataset) of training and validation sets, where
each validation set has one project. This validation method
is widely used in empirical studies when dealing with small
datasets [32].

Regarding the evaluation criteria, we decided to use several accuracy measures to assess the achieved estimations.
We used some summary measures [33], namely MMRE,
MdMRE, Prediction at level 25% (Pred(25)), that have
been and are widely employed by researchers investigating
measures and techniques for effort estimation. This can be
useful to allow for the comparison with other researches
that have been published in this context. Moreover, based
on the observation that some criticisms have been moved to

those summary measures [34], we also decided to employ
Mean of Absolute Residuals (MAR) and Median of Absolute
Residuals (MdAR). These measures are described in the
following.

The Magnitude of Relative Error (MRE) is defined as [33]

*MRE* = *[|]* *[Actual][ −]* *[Predicted]* *[|]* (2)

Moreover, we tested the statistical significance of the

better effort estimations than those achieved using MeanEFF

differences in the pairs is zero.

In order to have also an indication of the practical/managerial significance of the results we analyzed the
effect size [38]. Effect size is a simple way of quantifying
the standardized difference between two groups. It has many
advantages over using only the tests of statistical significance, since “whereas p-values reveal whether a finding is
statistically significant, effect size indicates practical significance” [38]. In particular, we employed the point-biserial
correlation *r* because it is suitable to compute the magnitude
of the difference when a non parametric test (e.g., Wilcoxon)
is used [39]. In the empirical software engineering field

[38], the magnitude of the effect sizes measured using the
point-biserial correlation is classified as follows: small (0 to
0.193), medium (0.193 to 0.456), and large (0.456 to 0.868).

achieved with nCFunP and CFPafp based models are about

question: i.e., *the early effort estimations obtained using*
*CFunP and AFP are statistically better than the estimations*
*obtained using baseline benchmarks.*

VI. T HREATS TO VALIDITY

It is widely recognized that several factors can bias
the construct, internal, external, and conclusion validity of
empirical studies [40] [28].

As for the construct validity, the collection of the information to determine size measures and actual effort represents
a crucial aspect. Often, this is the main issue to conduct this
kind of study [41]. As described in Section IV-B we have
supervised the procedure employed by the involved software
company to carefully collect the information we needed for

the empirical analysis. In particular, we tried to perform the
data collection task in a controlled and uniform fashion. Of

course we have to take into account that empirical studies do
not ensure the level of confidence achieved with controlled
experiments.

Some factors should be taken into account for the internal

validity: subjects’ authoring and designing experience, and
reliability of the data and lack of standardization [28] [35]

[40]. The managers involved in the study were professionals who worked in the software company. No initial
selection of the subjects was carried out, so no bias has
been apparently introduced. Moreover, the Web applications
were developed with technologies and methods that subjects
had experienced. Consequently, confounding effects from
the employed methods and tools can be excluded. As for
the reliability of the data and lack of standardization, the
questionnaires used were the same for all the Web applications and the project managers were instructed on how
to use the questionnaires, to correctly provide the required
information. Instrumentation effects in general did not occur
in this kind of studies. As for the conclusion validity we
carefully applied the estimation methods and the statistical
tests, verifying all the required assumptions.

With regard to the external validity, the applications
involved in our empirical analysis are representative samples
of modern Web applications, taking into account their type,
functionality, target platforms, and complexity. Thus, we
are confident that the type of analyzed Web applications
did not bias the validity of the achieved results. On the
other hand, it is recognized that the results obtained in an
industrial context might not hold in other contexts. Indeed,
each context might be characterized by some specific project
and human factors, such as development process, developer
experience, application domain, tools, technologies used,
time, and budget constraints [42].

VII. C ONCLUSION

The empirical analysis have highlighted that the considered COSMIC-based approximate countings provide good
early estimations, significantly better than those obtained
with baseline benchmarks. Moreover, the first counting
provides results better than the Average Functional Process
approach. Thus, we can conclude that the use of COSMICbased approximate countings resulted to be a suitable approach for early effort estimations. As expected the estimations obtained with the approximate sizes are worse
than those achieved employing the size resulted from the
application of the standard COSMIC method.

Since replications of a study, in different setting and
with larger datasets, are always required in this field, as
future work, we plan to collect and analyze data from other
companies. Furthermore, we intend to investigate the other
approximate sizing approaches proposed in the COSMIC
documentation [7]. In particular, we would like to analyze


pros and cons of the different approaches not only with
respect to the accuracy of the estimation but also with respect
to the information required to apply the methods and then
the time needed to carry out the measurement.

We are also planning to investigate whether the kind of
applications considered can influence the obtained estimation models.
