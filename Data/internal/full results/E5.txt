This paper focuses on effort prediction for Web design
and authoring processes. We adopt the classification
proposed by Lowe and Hall [3] where authoring

presentation. Design covers the methods used for

reused. In addition, several media metrics were adapted
from [6]. The Prediction model proposed was generated

**2.3 Validity of the Case Study**

There are three types of threats to the validity of an
empirical study [9]:

- Construct validity: Represents to what extent the
predictor and response variables precisely measure
the concepts they claim to measure.

- Internal validity: Represents to what extent
conclusions can be drawn about the causal effect of
the predictor variables on the response variable.

- External validity: Represents the domain to which a
study’s findings can be generalised.

**2.3.1 Construct Validity.** Design and authoring effort are
difficult concepts to measure. However, it is argued that
the data collected and the response variable derived are
intuitively reasonable measures.

- There is no standard on what constitutes the design
and authoring processes of a Web application.
Therefore, the activities used in this study may not be
representative of all practices employed in the Web
community.

**3. The Generalised Linear Model**

**3.1 Introduction**

Generalised linear models (GLMs) [7] provide a
flexible regression framework for predictive modelling of
effort. The models allow non-linear relationships between
response and predictor variables, and they admit a wide
range of choices for the distribution of the response
variable. They are therefore much more realistic than
conventional models such as ordinary least squares
regression.
In its most general form, a GLM consists of a response
variable, ***Y***, together with ***p*** predictor variables, ***x*** ***1*** ***,...,*** ***x*** ***p*** ***.***

For our purposes, the response is the total effort of a
project, while ***x*** ***1*** ***,...,*** ***x*** ***p*** represent metrics such as

application size or complexity. The response ***Y*** is
modelled with a systematic component and a random
component. The systematic component describes the
relationship between ***Y*** and the predictor variables, while
the random component expresses the random variation in
***Y*** from one project to the next.
For the random component, a probability distribution
is chosen for ***Y*** from the group of so-called exponential
distributions [7]. This group includes the familiar
Gaussian, Gamma, Poisson, and Binomial distributions.
The Gaussian distribution is used for ordinary least
squares regression. It assumes that the distribution of ***Y*** is
symmetric about its mean (i.e. ***Y*** is equally likely to fall
above or below the predicted value), and that ***Y*** has
constant variance for all projects. Other distributions in
the group, such as Poisson and Gamma, move away from
these assumptions and allow the variability of ***Y*** to
increase as the mean increases. The Gamma distribution,
furthermore, is not symmetric, but accommodates larger
variation above the mean than below it.
The systematic component of the GLM fixes the mean,
or location, of the distribution of ***Y***, by relating it to the
predictor variables ***x*** ***1*** ***,...,*** ***x*** ***p*** . Let ***Y*** ***i*** be the value of ***Y*** for

the ***i*** th project, and let **µ** ***i*** **= E(** ***Y*** ***i*** **)** denote the mean of the
distribution of ***Y*** ***i*** . The mean **E(** ***Y*** ***i*** **)** is also referred to as the
*predicted* effort for project ***i*** . The systematic component
involves a transformation of the mean, **E(** ***Y*** ***i*** **)**, using a
monotone *link function*, ***g***, that is invertible over the
whole real line. The transformed mean is modelled as a
linear function of the predictors:

Any GLM is specified by the two components above: a
probability distribution for ***Y***, and a transformation ***g*** .
Ordinary least squares regression is a special case of a
GLM, in which the error distribution of ***Y*** is Gaussian and
the link function ***g*** is the identity. The ordinary least
squares model is often criticised for its restrictive
assumptions [12], which are overcome by using the more
sophisticated models available in the GLM framework.
GLMs are implemented in most statistical software
packages. The analyses in this paper were conducted
using the software S-PLUS [13].

**3.2 Model for Effort Data**

We formulated the effort GLM as follows. The
variable ***Y*** was total effort, from Table 4. The predictor
variables were taken from the size, reusability, and
complexity metrics in Tables 1 to 3. We chose the
distribution for ***Y*** to be Gamma. The positive skew of this
distribution is particularly valuable for effort models,
because it allows for the possibility of severe overrun on a
time schedule. The variance of the Gamma distribution is
proportional to the square of the mean; so as the expected
duration of a project increases, the associated variability
also increases. In practice this means it is easier to
estimate a precise duration for a project lasting a few days
than it is for one lasting several weeks. Other authors
have also recommended the Gamma distribution for effort
estimation [14], although not in the GLM context.
With the Gamma distribution there are three usual
choices for the link function ***g*** : the identity link, ***g(x) = x*** ;
the logarithmic link, ***g(x) = log(x)*** ; and the inverse link,
***g(x) = 1/x*** . We investigated each of these links, but for
the Auckland data the choice was found to have very little
impact on the results, due to small magnitudes of
parameter estimates. The identity link was favoured, for
two reasons. Firstly, it provided the best fit of the three
models, by a small margin. Secondly, the identity link
suggests a linear relationship between the response
variable and the predictors. While a linear relationship is
often unrealistic, plots suggested that relationships were
in fact broadly linear for the Auckland data. It is
reasonable that the predicted effort should vary linearly
with many of the metrics in Tables 1 to 3, such as *page*
*count* and *total page allocation* . The choice of ***g*** depends


upon circumstances, however, and the authors favoured
the logarithmic link function when using alternative
metrics with a different set of data [15].
Selecting the predictor variables ***x*** ***1*** ***,...,*** ***x*** ***p*** is not

always straightforward, and some experimentation is
usually required. Chief problems are the small size of the
data set (37 observations), and relationships within the
variables. Ideally the predictors should not be linearly
related to each other: for example, it would be wrong to
include the metrics *page count*, *connectivity*, and
*cyclomatic* *complexity* together in the same model,
because the last of these is a linear function of the first
two. With some other variables the relationship might be
more subtle, and it is important to check for possible
hidden relationships between variables such as *page count*
and *total page allocation* .
With only 37 observations, it would be unwise to
include more than about 4 or 5 predictors in the model.
For this reason we recommend that a model selection
procedure is used to select variables. A popular basis for
model selection is Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC),
which seeks the best fit to the data while penalising
overparametrisation [16]. After considerable
experimentation, we found that the AIC criterion
consistently reduced our predictive model to the two
predictors ***x*** ***1*** = *total page allocation*, and ***x*** ***2*** = *total media*
*allocation* . These represent the strongest size metrics, and
the selection is reasonable. It is likely that the data set was
not large enough to allow the effects of the complexity
metrics to be detected.

**4. Prediction and Project Management**

The need for effective time management in an
organisation undertaking large numbers of projects was
underlined in [14], where it was pointed out that the risks
associated with each project must be absorbed into an
organisation-wide management plan. Attempts to
accommodate a project's risk within the budget of the
project itself would lead to greatly inflated time estimates
and uncompetitive contract bids. It was further suggested
in [14] that risk management could be effective only if all
projects undertaken by the organisation were of similar
size.
The GLM that we have used provides a very natural
framework for risk management, and it can accommodate
considerable variability in project sizes. The key lies in
the fact that the GLM, in common with all regression
models, estimates a full probability distribution for the
response variable ***Y*** . Instead of obtaining just a single
point estimate for project time, we can calculate the
probability that the time will exceed any given value.
With a point estimate of 20 days, for instance, the fitted
probability distribution of ***Y*** gives the probability of the
time exceeding 25 days, 30 days, and so on. Using this
knowledge for all proposed projects, we can determine
the required level of risk for each one in order to attain an
acceptable collective risk level across all projects.
Although we shall illustrate the risk procedure using
the Gamma GLM of Section 3, the methods can be
applied to the output from any regression model. We

**5. Conclusions and Future Work**

This paper has reported: i) a quantitative case study
evaluation (CSE) that measured several Web metrics,
grouped as size metrics, complexity metrics, structure
metrics, effort metrics and reuse metrics; ii) the proposal
of an effort prediction model which was generated using a
Generalised Linear Model (GLM); iii) the assessment of
the prediction power for the proposed model; iv) the use
of the GLM as a framework for risk management.
The AIC criterion consistently reduced the predictive

project can be calculated, using expression (3).
A nice property of the Gamma GLM of Section 3 is
that the chosen value of ***k*** places the same level of
security on every project. For example, in order to be 99%
certain that the total budget is not exceeded, the strategy
above might place an individual budget on each project
that is (say) 70% sure to be adequate. This makes the
policy easy to report and implement, since all projects are
treated in the same way. The property holds for all
Gamma and Gaussian GLMs, although not for Poisson
GLMs. Note that projects with the same security level do
not necessarily have the same time allotted to them; for
example, for one project the budgeted time at 70%
security might be 8 days, while for another project it
might be 20 days.
Figure 4 shows the relationship between the overall
security ***S*** and the security per project, ***s***, and some precise
readings from the figure are given in Table 6. The values
were obtained from the set of 37 projects to which the
original model was fitted, although in practice we would
usually wish to consider a new set of projects. Ideally the
curve should lie well below the line ***y=x,*** and this is the
case for ***S>0.6.***
The figure and table show that we do not need to be
excessively cautious in our individual bids in order to
attain a very high level of security across all projects
together. For example, to be 95% sure that all 37 projects
will be completed on time, we can budget at only 68.2%
security per project. This means that roughly one third of
the projects will overrun.
For the Auckland case study, the policy outlined in this
section would suggest an overall bid of 2069 hours for an
overall security level of ***S =*** **0.90** (requiring security per
project ***s*** **= 0.661** ). The total time observed was 1818

*media allocation* . Other metrics might have been selected
as well, had the dataset had more than 37 observations.
The GLM provides a natural framework for effort
prediction and risk management over a set of new
projects. The procedures outlined in Section 4 can be
applied with any regression model, and are robust to
moderate differences in project sizes.
GLMs are classified as algorithmic models [18].
Algorithmic models, which have been to date the most
popular in the literature, attempt to represent the
relationship between effort and one or more project
characteristics. The main “cost driver” used in such a
model is usually taken to be some notion of the software
size (e.g. the number of lines of source code, number of
pages, number of links).
Other existing approaches to effort prediction include
expert judgement and analogy [18].
An advantage of algorithmic models over estimation
and expert judgement is that they allow users to see how a
model derives its conclusions. This can be important for
the purpose of verification as well as theory building and
gaining an understanding of the process being modelled

[18]. In addition, these models need to be calibrated to the
local environment in which they are used, which
significantly improves their accuracy [2].
Recently several comparisons have been made
between the three categories of prediction models algorithmic, expert judgement and analogy - based on
their prediction power [19,20,21,22,23,24]. However no
convergence has been obtained to date.
As our future work we plan to apply effort prediction
to the whole development cycle of Web applications and
to compare the performance of GLMs against human
estimation and analogy.
To conclude, there is an urgent need for adequate Web
development effort prediction at an early stage in the
development. As the use of the Web as a resource
delivery environment increases, effort estimation can
contribute significantly to the reduction of costs and time
involved in developing Web applications.
